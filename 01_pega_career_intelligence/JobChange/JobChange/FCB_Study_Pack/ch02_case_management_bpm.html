2.1 A request needs a journey

A case is one unit of business work with a start, an end and a goal: "change the mailing address on Dana Whitfield's accounts". Marcus does not think in terms of rules or classes; he thinks in terms of work that arrives, is looked at, is approved or sent back, and is eventually finished. The first design question is therefore what the lifecycle is, and the second is what shape it takes when real operations work does not go in a straight line.

Three terms are used loosely and are worth separating at the start. A case type is the template, a rule that defines what every address-change case looks like and how it proceeds; for Riverbend it is FCB-Dep-Work-AccountMaintenance. A case instance is one actual case created from that template and stored as a work object: AM-10417, with a unique key (pzInsKey) and a display ID (pyID) built from the class group's prefix and a sequence. And the case status (pyStatusWork) is the business status that Marcus and Priya see, such as New or Pending-Approval, and that every change writes into history.

The simplest design is a sequence of screens: a form, a review page, an approve button. That is what "workflow" meant before case management frameworks, and it fails the first time reality intrudes. A document arrives late. A sanctions hit stops everything. A legal order arrives in the middle of a closure. A supervisor reassigns work because a colleague is away. A linear sequence cannot represent any of that, so case management models the goal and the lifecycle instead, lets the route change, and attaches tasks, deadlines, documents, audit and correspondence to the same record.

For AM-10417 the lifecycle has five phases, and Pega's name for a major phase is a stage. Intake captures the request and fetches what is needed. Verify decides how much scrutiny it deserves. Approve is where a second person decides. Fulfil updates the core and tells the customer. Resolve closes the case. Inside each stage, a process (implemented by a flow) runs a set of steps, and a step that needs a person creates an assignment: a task held either on one operator's worklist or in a shared work queue.

2.2 Stages, processes and steps

With the lifecycle sketched, the question is how a case actually moves from one stage to the next, and what happens when it does not go to the next one. Pega distinguishes three kinds of stage for exactly that reason.

A primary stage is part of the normal path: when every required process in it finishes, the case moves to the next primary stage, automatically by default. Intake, Verify, Approve and Fulfil are primary. An alternate stage is off the path: cancellation, rejection, an exception. It is entered explicitly, by a transition or a ticket, and is not part of the default sequence. A resolution stage is final: reaching it and finishing its process resolves the case. When Priya rejects Dana's change, the case does not simply stop; it enters an alternate stage called Rejected, which sets a resolved-rejected status and sends the customer a notice.

Stages carry a few settings that matter early. Entry and exit conditions let a stage be skipped when a condition is true, for example skipping the enhanced identity check for a low-risk change. A stage can be left to advance automatically or can wait for an explicit user action. Starting a stage sets the case status. A stage can have its own service level. And a stage can offer optional actions, ad hoc processes a user may run at any time while the case is in that stage. Transitions between stages are automatic (the last process finishes), manual (a user chooses "change stage" or a flow action moves the case to a named stage, typically a supervisor), skipped (the condition says the stage has nothing to do) or a jump to an alternate stage through a flow transition or ticket, from which the case may resume a primary stage.

Inside a stage, what a "step" is depends on what the step must do. Case Designer, the visual editor in App Studio (also available in Dev Studio), offers a vocabulary of step types, each of which maps onto a shape in the process's flow.

2.3 Flow shapes: what actually runs

A flow is a graph of shapes and connectors that defines the sequence of tasks in a process. Each shape either creates a task, performs automation, branches, pauses or ends. Sam builds the Verify process for AM-10417 by starting with the simplest version and adding shapes as the design needs them.

The first version is a start, an Assignment (Marcus reviews) and an end. The Assignment shape creates a task for a person or queue and waits for a flow action; its configuration carries the routing, the flow actions available, the service level, local actions, instructions and the screen to show. Before Marcus can review anything the case needs Dana's profile, so a call to the core goes in front of the assignment. An Integrator shape calls a connector (REST, SOAP or SQL) with request and response mapping and a declared error path, without writing activity code; a Utility shape runs an activity with no human involved and is for internal logic a data transform cannot express. The rule of thumb is Integrator for external calls and Utility for internal logic, and Utility shapes should stay thin.

Next the process must decide whether this change is sensitive. A Decision shape branches on a decision table, tree, when rule or expression, and its results map to outgoing connectors. When the logic is a decision table that the business should maintain, use a Decision shape; when there are two or three simple conditions, a Fork (branching on several when rules, first true or all, with an otherwise path) is lighter. Then a sensitive change needs a document, and the case should wait for it: a Wait shape pauses the flow until a time, a duration or a condition, including a case dependency (waiting for child cases) or a ticket. Finally a Notify shape sends correspondence or a notification, and an End shape terminates the flow or resolves the case with a status.

Three shapes handle work in parallel, and they are easy to confuse with one another. Split-For-Each runs a subflow once for each element of a page list, in parallel, with a join condition (all, any or a count), which suits one verification per signer. Split-Join runs two or more different subprocesses in parallel and rejoins, which suits a sanctions check and a tax-ID check at once. A Subprocess shape calls another flow, either to reuse it inside the same case or, with Create Case, to spawn a separate case. A Ticket shape, covered in 2.4, is a named exception that can be raised from anywhere. A Start shape is the entry point, with one per flow or several for different ways in.

FLOW SKETCH: Verify process for AM-10417 (Start) --> [Integrator: fetch Dana's profile] --ok--> <Decision: sensitive?> | error | yes | no v v v [Assignment: retry / manual fetch] [Assignment: request proof of address] (End) | received v [Assignment: Marcus reviews] --> (End) TICKET CUSTOMER_CANCEL (raised by an external event) jumps to the Cancelled alternate stage

Two sketches of confusion deserve a direct answer. Subprocess versus child case is a question of ownership and lifecycle: a subprocess runs in the same case and shares its data, whereas a child case is a separate case with its own lifecycle, audit, SLA and owner (2.7). Wait versus SLA is a question of what is doing the work: a Wait pauses the flow, while an SLA measures time and escalates but does not pause anything, and the right design for a document that may not arrive is both, a Wait for the reply with an SLA on the waiting step for follow-up reminders.

The ways this goes wrong are the ones that leave work stranded. A Utility containing a thirty-step activity is logic that belongs in rules. An Integrator with no error connector leaves the flow stalled in a "flow error" status, with the case turning up later in a broken-process report. Decision logic hard-coded into connectors as inline expressions is not maintainable by the business. And a Split-For-Each over hundreds of elements on one case should be a queue processor or child cases instead.

2.4 A late document and a rejection

The case is flagged sensitive, because Dana's address changed within 30 days of a card reissue request, and so the Verify process asks for a proof of address. Dana's document does not arrive when the review is supposed to happen. A case that simply waits is acceptable; a case that cannot be pulled back to an earlier point when something unexpected happens is not. Tickets and flow actions are how a flow stays flexible.

A ticket is a named marker in a flow, and also a named event. When it is raised, by an activity, a utility, a declarative rule, an external event or an SLA escalation, the flow in which the ticket lives resumes from the ticket point, wherever the case currently is. The reason it exists is that exceptions occur anywhere: without tickets, the flow would need a connector from every shape to the exception path. A ticket is defined as a Ticket rule, placed in the flow with a Ticket shape or attached to an assignment, and raised through a Set-Ticket method, an SLA escalation action, a declarative trigger, a "raise ticket" flow action or a Service-REST call that sets a ticket on an existing case. Raising it moves control to the ticket location and removes the current assignments of that flow, unless the ticket location is in a separate parallel branch. That last point is the one to remember: a raised ticket abandons whatever assignment was open.

For AM-10417 a ticket named CUSTOMER_CANCEL lets Dana withdraw the request from any stage. In a later chapter the same mechanism lets a legal order freeze an in-flight closure. Common uses are jump-back after rework, cancellation, a hold, a regulatory stop and an SLA breach path. The pitfalls follow directly: reuse of one ticket name for different meanings across flows, a ticket that jumps into the middle of a stage that depends on data not yet set, forgetting that the current assignment is dropped, and failing to write a history entry and a status explaining why. Always do the last two.

Now Marcus has the proof of address, and he reviews and submits the case for approval. He does this through a flow action, the rule that defines what a user can do on an assignment: the form shown, and the processing before and after. A connector action sends the flow down a named outgoing connector (approve, reject, return) when submitted, which is how decisions are made. A local action performs an operation within the same step, such as adding a note, and returns to the same assignment without advancing. The flow action also names the screen, a validate rule that runs on submit (a failure returns messages and keeps the user on the form), pre-processing that runs before the form appears (to prefill it), post-processing that runs after validation (to set status or compute values), and the privilege needed to see the action.

Priya opens the assignment on Monday afternoon and rejects it: the proof of address is a photograph too small to read. Her flow action's reject connector sends the case back to Marcus, with the status changed and a history entry recording her reason. Marcus asks Dana for a clearer copy, resubmits, and Priya approves on Tuesday 3 March. Nothing about this required the flow to know in advance that a rejection might occur; the reject connector and the return path were defined once, and the case type carries any number of rejections. What it does require is that the reason is captured, since a rejection that leaves no trace is exactly the evidence gap an auditor looks for.

Check your understanding.

A legal order against Dana's account arrives while AC-10882 is waiting for Priya's approval, and a ticket is raised on the closure case. What happens to Priya's open assignment, and what should be recorded?

Answer. The raised ticket moves control to the ticket point in the closure flow and removes the current assignments of that flow, so Priya's approval assignment is dropped and the case resumes at the legal-hold review step. A history entry and a status must explain why, or Priya and the auditor will see an approval that vanished with no reason. This assumes the ticket location is in the same flow branch as the assignment; if it sits in a separate parallel branch, the assignment would survive, which is why ticket placement is a design decision and not a detail.

2.5 Who sees the work: routing and work queues

The assignment that Marcus reviews has to reach Marcus. Routing is the mechanism that decides who sees a task, and it exists because operations teams are organised into queues, skill groups, shifts and authority levels. Each assignment must land with someone entitled and able to do it, and must be redistributable when volume or availability changes.

The first decision is between putting an assignment on a person's worklist and in a shared work queue (a work basket). A worklist assignment has one named owner from the start, which suits case ownership and relationship-based work, but it is a push model that needs balancing logic and strands the work if that person is on leave. A work queue has no owner until someone takes the item; users pull the next item with Get Next Work, which ranks by urgency (2.8), and the arrangement survives absences and gives a clear owner once the item is taken. For operations volume work the queue is almost always the right answer, which is why Riverbend routes the address-change review to DepOps-Maintenance.

The standard routers on an Assignment shape cover the usual cases: to a specific operator's worklist; to a named work queue; to a skilled group, chosen from the skills configured on operator IDs, which is how legal orders reach people with a legal-process skill; to the manager of the case's work group; back to the current operator, the originator or the creator, for rework; by a decision table or custom router activity, for routing by authority level, product line or state; and up a reporting structure for approvals. The list varies slightly by release, so confirm it on the Assignment shape's properties in the version you use.

Routing decisions often depend on more than the router type: a required skill and minimum rating on the operator, availability (an out-of-office setting that delegates work), workload (least-workload routing), authority (an approval limit on the operator or in an authority matrix table) and shift or region (queues by region with a calendar defining business hours). A queue design for Riverbend's deposit operations shows how those choices combine.

DEPOSIT OPS WORK QUEUE DESIGN (illustrative) DepOps-Intake : new requests, general maintenance (all L1 ops) DepOps-Maintenance : address/signer/beneficiary changes (mreed works here) DepOps-Closure : account closure checks and fulfilment DepOps-Payments-Exc : ACH returns (R01/R02/R03), stop pay, wire exceptions DepOps-Legal : garnishments, tax levies, subpoenas, court freezes (skill: LegalProcess) DepOps-Compliance : OFAC hits, KYC/AML refreshes (skill: Compliance) DepOps-Escheat : dormancy/unclaimed property DepOps-Recon : reconciliation breaks DepOps-Supervisor : escalations, SLA breaches, approvals above L1 authority (praman) Operator -> Work Group (team) -> Access Group (application/roles) -> Skills (operator ID) -> Work queues accessible

A queue is only as good as the operators who can reach it. The failure mode that costs most is a work queue that no active operator can access, so assignments sit forever and SLAs breach with nobody noticing; the defence is an alert on queues that have no eligible operator during business hours. The other recurring mistakes are hard-coding operator IDs or routing by name in a flow, using worklist routing for volume work, and writing custom router activities that run a report for every assignment created, which is slow under load.

2.6 Approval, and why Marcus cannot approve his own change

Marcus has prepared the change and could, if the design allowed it, also approve it. In a bank that is precisely what must not be possible. A person who initiates a sensitive change cannot also authorise it, and higher-value or higher-risk actions need higher authority: closing an account with a large balance, releasing a legal hold, overriding a sanctions alert, waiving a fee, changing a signer. Approval is therefore a design topic of its own, with several patterns.

The simplest is a single approval: one approver decides, through an Approve/Reject assignment routed to a queue or a manager. Cascading approval escalates up a chain until someone has enough authority, configured with an authority or reporting structure or as a loop in the flow that checks a limit. Reporting-structure approval routes to the operator's manager and then that manager's manager, using the "reports to" hierarchy. An authority matrix is a table mapping product, amount and risk to the required approver level, implemented as a decision table or decision data looked up through a data page. Parallel approval requires two or more approvers, using Split-Join or an approval step with several approvers and a join condition. And maker-checker, the four-eyes principle, requires that a different person approves from the one who entered the change.

Maker-checker is the pattern that gets interviewed on, and the instructive point is that routing alone does not implement it. If the only control is a queue that the maker is not supposed to be in, then a reassignment, an ad hoc action or an API call can bypass it. The control has to be enforced where it cannot be bypassed, in security rules and server-side validation, and so the design has several layers.

1. At Capture, store maker: .MakerOperatorID = pxRequestor.pyUserIdentifier (data transform, post-processing of the maker flow action) 2. Checker assignment routing: to work queue DepOps-Approvers OR skilled group with Checker skill 3. Hard control (cannot rely on UI alone): a) Access Deny / Access When rule: deny "Approve" privilege when pxRequestor.pyUserIdentifier = .MakerOperatorID b) Flow action pre-processing/validation: a validate rule returns "Maker cannot approve own case" c) Get Next Work exclusion: a when rule on the assignment so that the maker is not offered the item 4. Record: history entry with maker, checker, decision, timestamp; field-level audit on amount and status 5. Authority: checker's approval limit >= case amount, otherwise cascade to the next level

For AM-10417 this means that when Marcus submits the review, the maker's identifier is stored on the case; Priya's approval assignment routes to the approvers' queue; and if Marcus somehow reached the assignment, the Access Deny on the approve privilege and the server-side validation would both refuse. Chapter 10 builds the access model that makes those rules work and tests them role by role. Each layer closes a gap the previous one leaves: routing keeps the item out of the maker's view, the security rule refuses the action even if reached, validation covers API and ad hoc paths, and a daily report of any case where maker equals checker (target zero) detects what the others missed.

2.7 Parallel work: child cases and Split-Join

Some work does not go in a line. When Dana later closes CHK-8842-0117, several things must be checked at once: a sanctions re-screen, whether a legal hold exists, and whether recurring ACH or bill-pay items depend on the account. And if the account had five joint owners, each owner's confirmation would be a separate piece of work. The question for the designer is where that parallel work lives, and Pega gives two answers with different consequences.

The first is to keep it inside the case: a Split-Join runs the different checks as parallel branches of the parent's flow, and a Split-For-Each runs one subflow per element of a list. They share the parent's clipboard and the parent's single status, and all branches write to the same case, so the parent lock is a single contention point. They are right for short tasks done by the same team on tightly coupled data. The second is a child case, created through a subprocess that creates a case: a separate case with its own data, lifecycle, SLA, owner, audit, security and status, a separate row, searchable and reportable on its own.

A short sequence of questions settles most cases. Does the sub-work have a different owner or its own SLA? Would an auditor want to find it on its own? Can it outlive or start independently of the parent? If yes to any, it is a child case. Is it a short, same-team, same-data task? Then Split-Join or a subprocess. Is the number of parallel items large, fifty or more? Then child cases or a queue processor, never a Split-For-Each on one case.

Riverbend's closure has two instances of each answer. The five-owner confirmation could be a Split-For-Each if it is a simple electronic signature check by the same person, but child cases ("Owner Confirmation") if each owner has a separate SLA and a compliance review by a different team. A legal order that restrains several accounts spawns one "Hold Placement" child case per account, so that each account's core update, success or failure and audit stands alone: one account failing to update does not stall the others, there is proof of the exact hold time for each, an individual case can be reprocessed without re-running the order, and the parent is not locked while the children work.

The anti-pattern to avoid is the parent case being updated from many children at the moment of resolution, which creates parent-lock contention and "lock held" errors. Children write their own results, and the parent waits on case dependency and reads child statuses afterward by report or data page.

2.8 SLAs, urgency and how an SLA actually fires

Priya cannot watch every case. The business has internal commitments (Riverbend's address-change review has a four-business-hour goal and a one-business-day deadline, assumed Riverbend policy) and the bank has external deadlines whose clocks it does not control. What is needed is automated, auditable escalation in place of supervisors watching spreadsheets. A service level rule (Rule-Obj-ServiceLevel) defines how long work should take and what happens as time passes.

Three intervals

A service level has three intervals, and the design question for each is what should happen when it arrives. The goal is the desired completion time: the usual escalation is to notify the assignee and raise urgency slightly. The deadline is the time by which the work must complete: notify the supervisor, raise urgency substantially, perhaps reassign to the supervisor queue. The passed deadline follows the deadline and can recur at an interval: keep escalating, notify the manager and compliance, raise urgency each cycle, set a ticket. Each interval's actions can notify, escalate to another assignee, advance the flow, run an activity, set urgency or set a ticket.

The rule has a handful of options that make it usable in practice. The time can be a fixed interval from assignment creation or computed from a property, such as a case due date or regulatory deadline. A calendar makes it count only working time. Each interval carries an urgency value. The passed-deadline interval can repeat every N units, with a maximum count or until cleared, and a cap matters because an uncapped recurrence floods supervisors with email. Where it applies is a question of scope: on an Assignment shape's service level field (timer starts when the assignment is created and stops when it completes), on the case or a stage (a clock for the whole case or stage, for end-to-end turnaround), or on a Case Designer step, which sets that step's assignment SLA.

Calendars deserve a paragraph because they are where regulatory SLAs go wrong. A calendar rule defines working days, hours and holidays, and an SLA set to use it counts only business time. An SLA written as "three days" without a calendar crosses weekends and holidays, and a regulatory deadline in business days then becomes wrong. No single holiday list fits every regulatory clock: Reg E, Reg CC, the ACH network and state legal-order deadlines each use a different calendar, so the calendar is an input to a deadline, not a global (Chapter 12 §12.10). Time zone matters too: SLA calculations use the calendar's or the operator's time zone depending on configuration, and for a bank with operations in India and the US the design needs explicit calendars, a separate one per regulatory clock and another for staffing and shift SLAs (see Chapter 12 §12.10), with timestamps stored in UTC and displayed in the user's zone.

Urgency: the number that ranks work

Urgency is an integer from 0 to 100 that ranks work. It drives worklist order and the Get Next Work selection algorithm. Values above 100 are not stored: the system ignores adjustments that would push it past 100. There are two urgencies, and conflating them is the classic interview slip: case urgency is the priority of the case, assignment urgency is the priority of one piece of work on it.

The last four are documented but rarely used in practice. The two you will actually configure are the case type's Initial Urgency (Settings > General, default 10) and .pyUrgencyWorkAdjust.

Case urgency does not propagate to assignments that already exist. Pega's documentation is explicit: .pxUrgencyAssign reflects a manual adjustment to case urgency only if the adjustment is made before the assignment is created, not after. .pxUrgencyWork is copied into the assignment's total at creation time; raising case urgency later moves the case, not the open assignment sitting in somebody's worklist. If you need an existing assignment to jump the queue, adjust .pyUrgencyAssignAdjust on the assignment. It is the thing that bites teams: "we escalated the case and the work still did not come up next in Get Next Work."

Runtime: the system recalculates urgency when the value of a standard urgency property changes, or when a user saves the case.

The arithmetic is easiest to follow on one case with a statutory deadline. A legal-order case has a case type Initial Urgency of 30 (above the default 10, because these carry deadlines). A supervisor raises case urgency by 10 before the review assignment is created. The review assignment's SLA has initial urgency 10, plus 10 at goal and plus 30 at deadline, with no manual adjustment yet.

Urgency is also a different thing from the other two words in the neighbourhood. A service level is a time rule with actions and is not the number you sort by. Urgency is a numeric ranking value, often the outcome of SLA progression, and is not time. Priority is a business label (high, medium, low) that users or integrations supply; it is not an engine field by itself, and you map it into an initial urgency.

How an SLA actually fires

An SLA does not run in a thread. When an assignment with a service level is created, Pega computes the next due time and writes an entry to the SLA queue, specifically the System-Queue-ServiceLevel table, not a sleeping process. A system-managed background processor then runs periodically: it selects entries whose next-action time has arrived, runs the escalation actions for that interval, updates urgency and re-queues the entry for the next interval. When the assignment completes, its timer entry is removed.

Assignment created (SLA Goal 4h, Deadline 8h, Passed every 2h) | v engine writes SLA timer entry (next action time = created + 4h) +--------------------------------------------------------------+ | SLA queue / timeout store (sorted by next action time) | +--------------------------------------------------------------+ | ^ re-queue next interval v | SLA processing (batch requestor) wakes every cycle -> selects due entries -> loads the case -> runs escalation actions (Notify, Urgency, Reassign, Ticket) -> history row "Goal passed" / "Deadline passed" -> on assignment completion: timer entry removed

The legacy agent and the queue processor are different execution models (agent schedule versus queue-processor dequeue). Do not assume every SLA-like timer in the platform uses System-Queue-ServiceLevel: case-level, stage-level and step-level SLAs, and timer and wait shapes, are configured separately and may be serviced by different background mechanisms. Verify per release rather than generalising from assignment SLAs. Which of them services assignment SLAs on a given system is something to read, not to assume, and the table pairs each statement with the screen that proves it.

What this means for Lena is a diagnosis she can state in advance. If the SLA processor stops, no escalation emails go out, urgency does not rise, supervisors say cases "never turned red" though they are days overdue, .pxUrgencyAssign is unchanged and history lacks "Goal passed" while the SLA backlog count grows. The typical causes are the processor stopped or disabled, a node type that does not include background processing so no node runs it, a stuck partition after an infrastructure fault, a poison entry (a case whose escalation activity throws) retrying and consuming capacity, a database lock on the SLA table, clock skew, or a deployment that replaced node classification. She diagnoses from the Queue Processors landing page (status, delayed and failed items, last run time), checks which nodes run it, reads the logs for exceptions in the escalation activity and looks at the broken queue items. Recovery is to restart the processor or fix the node classification and resolve or requeue broken items; once it catches up it fires every past-due interval at once, which produces an escalation email burst to supervisors, so communicate before restarting and consider throttling notification actions. Prevention is an alert on the delayed-items count and the oldest-item age, a synthetic SLA test case in production, tiny idempotent escalation activities and a written runbook.

Riverbend's own SLA designs follow from these mechanisms, all of them invented policy. The ACH return handling has an assignment SLA with a goal of two business hours and a deadline per reason, well inside the network deadline, because the return window is a business-day deadline set outside Pega and the SLA only provides earlier internal warnings assumed Riverbend policy. A Reg CC hold exception review has a case SLA on the Reg CC business-day calendar with a one-business-day goal and a two-day deadline, and a passed deadline that notifies the supervisor every four hours assumed Riverbend policy. A legal order has a case SLA computed from the order's response-due date, dynamic from a property, with the validation goal at due date minus five business days and a deadline at due date minus one assumed Riverbend policy. A Reg E dispute's investigation clock is in business days from the notice, as a stage-level SLA. An account closure has a case deadline of ten business days. Regulatory durations belong to Compliance; these are design patterns.

The things that go wrong with SLAs are consistent: actions that call slow integrations, an SLA in calendar days for a business-day regulation, a passed-deadline recurrence with no cap, an SLA on every step so that the processor's load multiplies, and an escalation activity that updates a parent case from thousands of children at once.

2.9 Correspondence: the confirmation letter

The change is approved and fulfilled, and Dana has to be told. A bank must send specific notices (closure confirmations, adverse action, legal-order acknowledgements, dormancy warnings, Reg DD notices for CD maturity), and they must be correct, consistent, reproducible and archived. Typing text into an activity does none of that. Correspondence is the generation and delivery of customer-facing or internal communications from a case, and it is a set of rules for that reason.

A correspondence rule (Rule-Obj-Corr) exists per channel. Email correspondence is an HTML body with embedded properties, From, To, CC, BCC, subject and attachments, sent through an Email Account rule (SMTP). Mail (print) is HTML formatted for print, rendered to a PDF attached to the case and handed to a print vendor by file or API; it is used for legal notices. Fax is similar and rare. SMS is a short text body delivered through a gateway or connector, and it needs opt-in and consent handling. Notice that the channels differ in what they need from the rest of the application: a letter needs a stored copy and a vendor handoff, an SMS needs consent.

A letter is assembled from reusable parts. A correspondence fragment is a reusable block such as the bank header, footer, legal disclosure or signature, included by name. A paragraph rule holds reusable text, and a field value holds localised labels and messages, one per language. Merge properties insert the case's data into the body, and the editor offers an insert-property tool that should be used in preference to typing the syntax from memory. An Email Account rule defines the SMTP and IMAP host, port, credentials through an authentication profile and the sender, and serves both outbound correspondence and the Email Listener inbound.

It helps to separate formal correspondence from a notification. A correspondence rule produces a formal customer document, a letter or legal notice, with full HTML and fragments, stored as a case attachment and correspondence history. A notification (a Notify shape or notification rule) is a lightweight alert to users or customers across channels, one template per channel with limited formatting, and the record is a delivery record. Riverbend uses correspondence for the closure confirmation letter, the legal-order acknowledgement and the dormancy notice, and notifications for "case assigned" or "SLA warning" alerts.

When the Fulfil stage of AM-10417 reaches its Notify step, the runtime sequence is worth following, because it explains where this can fail. A correspondence rule is selected by name and class, and rule resolution (Chapter 01) decides which variant applies, by language ruleset and circumstance by state or product. Fragments resolve and the case's data merges into the placeholders. The To and CC addresses come from the customer data page and an internal group address. The HTML is rendered, as an email or as a PDF. An email goes by SMTP through the Email Account (asynchronously through a queue processor when configured); a letter's PDF is stored on the case and sent by file or API to the print vendor. A correspondence history entry is written and a copy attached to the case through a link-attachment, with a sent or failed status. A failure follows a retry policy and raises an error notification to an operations queue and a case note.

Attachments and PDFs have their own practicalities. Case attachments are stored as link-attachment instances pointing at a file in the database, in a repository (cloud or file storage), or in a content management system; for a bank, an enterprise repository is preferable for retention and size, keeping the database for small items. PDF generation uses Pega's HTML-to-PDF engine, so fonts, page breaks, non-Latin characters and large tables all need testing. Localisation puts language-specific text in field values and paragraphs in language rulesets, selected by locale or by an explicit language property on the customer; dual-language notices are either two correspondence rules sent together or one rule composed of two fragments chosen by a customer preference, and whether both are legally required is a decision for Compliance. Regulatory text comes from Compliance and is versioned, never translated by the developer.

Bounces arrive later than the send. SMTP accepts the message and the bounce comes back as an email, which an Email Listener on the sending or a bounce mailbox can read, parse for the case ID in a header or subject token, and use to write a note or reopen an undeliverable task. A hard bounce (a bad address) sets a flag on the customer contact and triggers a fallback, a letter by mail and an operations task to verify the address, which for Dana's case is an address-update case of its own; a soft bounce (a full mailbox) retries.

2.10 Audit, reporting and bulk actions: what Priya sees

By Tuesday afternoon AM-10417 has produced a history of its own, and Priya wants to see more than one case: the open cases by queue and status, which are late, and which approvals are waiting. This is the reporting side of case management, and it rests on the audit the case already carries.

Every case has a History tab backed by history instances. For compliance traceability the history should show who created the case, who edited which field, who approved, which rule decided, which correspondence went out and which SLA events fired. That depends on meaningful history descriptions (include the old and new decision values, not just "Approved"), field-level audit on regulated fields, decision evidence attached to the case (screening results, a summary of a response payload, correspondence PDFs, never raw secrets), and a retention and purge policy with a read-only role for auditors.

Reports are built from the Report Definition rule: an Applies-To class, data access (class joins), columns (properties, functions, aggregates), filters (fixed, parameterised or user-prompted), sort, chart, pagination and a Query tab that shows the SQL. Two constraints matter before any dashboard is designed. Filters and joins on large tables should use exposed, indexed columns, because a filter on an unexposed property means scanning BLOB columns; and joins must be on exposed columns, with embedded page lists requiring a Declare Index (Chapter 03 explains why). Aggregates (count, sum, average, min, max, grouped) use the report's summarise mode, and charts are set on the report's chart tab. Users run and save reports from the Report Browser, and reports can be scheduled and emailed. Global case search is a different mechanism, a full-text search index separate from SQL reports.

SUPERVISOR DASHBOARD (Deposits Ops) built from Report Definitions + charts on a landing page Widget 1 Open cases by queue and status Widget 2 Cases past deadline (pxUrgencyAssign above a threshold, or SLA deadline passed) Widget 3 Average cycle time by case type (resolved last 30 days) Widget 4 Legal orders due in the next 3 business days Widget 5 ACH returns by reason code today vs the 7-day average Widget 6 Maker-checker exceptions (maker = checker) -> should always be zero Widget 7 Queue processor health (delayed items)

The pitfalls are the ones that make a dashboard slow or dangerous: reports filtered on unexposed properties, reports without date filters on large work tables, a count over millions of rows on a landing page that refreshes each time, and users allowed to build unrestricted reports against the work table, which is a performance and a personal-data risk. The design habit that avoids them is to expose or index any property used in a filter, join or sort from day one, and to give supervisors curated reports instead of letting every team write its own.

Priya's last tool is the bulk action. From the worklist or a work queue, selecting several assignments allows a bulk transfer (reassign to another operator or queue), a bulk approve or a configured bulk flow action: reassigning two hundred cases when staff are absent, or approving low-risk items at end of day where policy allows. Bulk actions run assignment by assignment with locking, so large batches belong in a queue processor with parameters, not the UI. They are controlled by privilege, with each item audited in history. A bulk approve contradicts maker-checker if it lets a maker approve their own items, so it must go through the same access rules.

2.14 Interview Q&A

Q1. Explain case, stage, process and step in Pega.

"A case type is the template for a business request. It is divided into stages, which are the lifecycle phases: primary stages in order, alternate stages for off-path scenarios like cancellation, and a resolution stage. Each stage holds processes, which are flows, and processes are made of steps such as assignments, automation, approvals or child cases. At runtime there is a case instance with an ID and a status, and assignments that sit on a worklist or a work queue."

Q2. Child case or Split-Join?

"If the sub-work has a different owner or its own SLA, an auditor would want to find it separately, or it can start or finish independently, it is a child case with its own lifecycle and audit. If it is a short, same-team task on tightly coupled data, a Split-Join or subprocess in the same case is enough. For large numbers of parallel items I use child cases or a queue processor, never a Split-For-Each on one case. I avoid children updating the parent at the same moment, because of parent lock contention."

Q3. Worklist or work queue?

"A worklist has one named owner from the start and suits relationship-based work, but it strands work when that person is away. A work queue is shared and pulled with Get Next Work, ranked by urgency, which suits operational volume. I define queues by function with skills on the sensitive ones, take authority from reference data, and alert on a queue with no eligible operator."

Q4. How do you implement maker-checker?

"I store the maker's operator ID at submit and route approval to a checker queue that excludes the maker. Routing alone can be bypassed by reassignment or an API call, so I enforce it in security: an Access Deny with a when condition on the approve privilege when the maker equals the current operator, a validate rule on the approve action so APIs are covered, and a daily report of any case where maker equals checker, target zero. Authority limits come from a decision table by amount and risk."

Q5. Explain SLA intervals and urgency.

"A service level has goal, deadline and passed-deadline intervals; each can raise urgency and run actions such as notify, reassign or raise a ticket, and passed deadline can recur. Urgency is an integer from 0 to 100. The ranking property is assignment urgency, pxUrgencyAssign: case urgency, pxUrgencyWork, copied in when the assignment is created, plus the assignment SLA's contribution, plus any manual adjustment. It drives worklist order and Get Next Work. A later change to case urgency does not reach an assignment that already exists, so to promote open work you adjust the assignment. SLA is time, urgency is ranking, and priority is a business label that I map into urgency."

Q6. How does an SLA actually fire in the engine?

"When an assignment with a service level is created, the engine records the next due time in the SLA queue, System-Queue-ServiceLevel. A background processor, historically the ServiceLevelEvents agent or a queue processor in releases that ship one, selected by the UseSLAQueueProcessor setting, picks up due entries, runs the interval's actions, updates urgency and re-queues the next interval. I would not assume which is active: I read the setting and the Queue Processors landing page on the deployed version, and I would not run both at once. Case, stage and step SLAs and timer shapes are configured separately, so I would not generalise from assignment SLAs."

Q7. SLAs have stopped escalating in production. What do you do?

"I check whatever services SLAs in Admin Studio: is it running, on which nodes, how many delayed and failed items, how old is the oldest. Then logs and PDC alerts for exceptions in the escalation activity, which can create a poison item, and node classification and database health. I fix the cause, restart, handle broken items, and warn supervisors because on catching up it fires all missed intervals and sends a burst of notifications. Afterwards I add a delayed-item alert and a runbook."

Q8. How do you handle business days and time zones in SLAs?

"An SLA that counts business time uses a calendar rule, and no single holiday list fits every regulatory clock, so I model the calendar as an input to the deadline: a separate calendar per regulatory clock and another for staffing. Timestamps are stored in UTC and shown in the user's zone. I never put a regulatory deadline in calendar days unless the regulation says calendar days."

Q9. What is a ticket and when would you use one?

"A named marker in a flow that, when raised, moves the flow to the ticket point from wherever the case is, dropping the current assignments of that flow. I use it for exceptions that can occur anywhere, such as customer cancellation, a legal freeze or an SLA breach path, and I always write a history entry and status explaining why it was raised."

Q10. How would you design the correspondence for a notice?

"A correspondence rule per channel with shared fragments for the header and legal text, selected by language and state through ruleset and circumstance, merge properties from the case and the customer data page, generated at fulfilment by a notify step rather than synchronously in a flow action. A copy is attached to the case, failures route to an ops queue, and bounces are handled by an email listener that flags the contact and triggers a mail fallback. Compliance owns approved wording and versions."

2.Z Where we are now

What the application can do

Dana's request now travels. It is created from the web channel, captured, checked for sensitivity, held for a document, reviewed by Marcus in DepOps-Maintenance, rejected once and then approved by Priya, who cannot be the maker, updated in the core, confirmed by letter, and resolved. Every step has a deadline and an escalation, and the case's audit and the supervisor's dashboard are built from the same facts.

The mechanisms behind it

Stages carry the lifecycle and processes carry the work, built from flow shapes; tickets and flow actions let the route bend; routing places assignments on queues, worklists and skills; maker-checker is enforced in security and validation, not only routing; child cases and Split-Join carry parallel work; a service level writes entries to an SLA queue that a background processor works through, and urgency is the bounded sum that ranks the result; correspondence is assembled from rules and archived on the case.

What it still cannot do

The case runs, but it has no real data. The review screen needs Dana's address and balance, and the fulfilment step needs the same facts; those live in the core, and the case does not own them. How the case obtains them once, keeps them correct while the case sits in Priya's queue, and writes a change back is the subject of Chapter 03.
