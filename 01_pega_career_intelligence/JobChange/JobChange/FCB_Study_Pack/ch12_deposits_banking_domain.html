12.1 An account's life, before any rule

Dana Whitfield's checking account CHK-8842-0117 and savings account SAV-8842-0233 are, to the bank, two liabilities: money Riverbend owes her on demand. Almost everything a deposits operations team does follows from one fact about that liability: it moves through a life, and at each stage of that life something can go wrong in a way that the law or the account agreement has a rule for. Understanding the stages first makes the rules arrive in context instead of as a list to memorise, and it is also the structure the case design follows, because each stage is a candidate case type or stage.

The account is originated when a customer applies and a product is chosen. It must be identified: the bank verifies who the customer is, screens them against sanctions and watch lists, and rates the risk. It is funded, usually by a first deposit that may be subject to a hold, and activated with an account number, signature or e-signature, card and online access. Then it is serviced for years: addresses change, signers and beneficiaries are added, payments arrive and leave, and exceptions occur (returns, overdrafts, stop payments, disputes, fraud alerts). Along the way funds can be held or restrained by a bank decision or by a legal order. If the customer stops using it, the account may become dormant and, if still unclaimed after the statutory period, its funds are remitted to the state. Otherwise it is closed, the remaining balance disbursed, and a post-closure tail of returned items, tax forms and record retention follows. Dana's address change AM-10417 in the earlier chapters was one servicing event; her closure AC-10882 is the end of the account's life, and four journeys through this chapter touch stages in between.

How the lifecycle becomes Pega case design. Not one giant case: a family of case types (Maintenance, Closure, Legal Order, Payment Exception, KYC Review, Reconciliation Break, Dormancy, Dispute) sharing a canonical data model, a common authority matrix and correspondence framework, and linked by keys (account number, customer ID). Cases interact: a legal order raises a ticket on an in-flight closure (Chapter 02 §2.4), and a dormancy case checks for active holds.

How the chapter treats numbers and policy

Regulatory rules change, differ by state and are interpreted by each bank's Compliance and Legal teams, so the chapter states the shape of each rule (what triggers it, who must act, in what order) throughout, and gives a specific number only where it cites a primary source: the Reg E investigation clocks and business-day definition, the Reg CC business-day definition and availability schedule with its amounts, the single check-processing region, the federal tax-levy holding period and the Nacha return-rate figures. Everywhere else a number is held as reference data owned by Compliance and is not stated. Two kinds of block keep law and policy visibly apart. A Legal requirement block (blue) states what a regulation requires with its citation. A Riverbend policy block (amber) states an invented internal target, always labelled as assumed and not a legal requirement. No state-by-state exemption tables are given, because they vary and change, and nothing here is legal advice.

12.2 Banking fundamentals and where Deposits and Shared Services sits

Dana's $4,418.55 in checking is not Riverbend's money: it is a debt Riverbend owes her, repayable on demand. That one fact, that a deposit is a liability of the bank, explains most of what follows. A bank takes money from depositors and lends or invests it, so its balance sheet has assets (what it owns or is owed: loans, securities, cash and reserves at the central bank), liabilities (what it owes, mainly customer deposits plus borrowings) and equity (the owners' stake, the cushion that absorbs losses). When Dana deposits 1,000, the bank owes her 1,000.

The profit comes from the spread. A deposit that pays 0.5 percent funds a loan that earns 6 percent, and the difference, measured against earning assets, is the net interest margin and the core profit engine. That is why deposits are the cheapest and most stable funding a bank has, and why losing them (an account closure, attrition after a bad experience) directly raises the bank's cost of funds. It is also why stable, relationship-based balances such as payroll and operating accounts, the core deposits, are the ones service quality protects. Interest paid on deposits is the cost of funds, so a rate exception or a CD rollover decision has a profit-and-loss effect, and the bank's ability to meet withdrawals is a liquidity matter that regulators set requirements for. Holds, availability policy and wire cut-offs interact with that liquidity management.

Two further things follow from the liability. Deposits are insured, in the United States by the FDIC, up to a limit per depositor, per insured bank and per ownership category; the standard maximum is set by FDIC rules and is held as reference data, not hard-coded. Because coverage depends on how an account is owned, a title change or a beneficiary update has insurance consequences, which is why the operations team treats them as controlled changes. And deposit products are heavily regulated (consumer protection, anti-money-laundering, funds availability, disclosure), so most deposit operations exist to execute regulated processes correctly and to prove it.

Who the customer is changes the work. Retail customers such as Dana have high volume and small balances, with the strongest consumer protections (Reg E, Reg DD) and heavy compliance timers. A small business needs entity documents, authorised signers and beneficial-ownership checks, while a commercial or corporate client such as Harlow Freight Co. brings large balances, treasury services (lockbox, sweeps, ACH and wire origination, positive pay) and bespoke arrangements, at lower volume per client. Wealth and fiduciary accounts are driven by legal documents and specialist handling. The comparison of segments is in the reference catalogue (12.18).

A bank is organised into business lines that own customers and products (retail banking, commercial banking, wealth) and shared services or operations that perform the processing common to many lines, so it is done once, consistently and at scale. Deposit operations and shared services typically include account opening and KYC support, account maintenance, payments operations and exceptions, legal and compliance processing, reconciliation, dormancy and escheatment, customer service operations and, adjacent to them, fraud operations; the exact structure varies by bank, so how a given team is organised is something to ask and not assume. Dana's address change lands in account maintenance, her dispute in payments exceptions, the hold on Victor's account in legal processing. The case engine sits in the middle of all of them.

12.3 Deposit product taxonomy

Dana's two accounts are different products with different rules, and the difference decides which processes can touch them. Her checking account (a demand deposit account, payable on demand) is the workhorse: paychecks arrive in it, bills and card purchases leave it, and so it carries the largest volume of maintenance, holds, ACH exceptions, stop payments, overdraft and disputes. Her savings account exists to accumulate interest, so it brings Reg DD disclosure (the interest rate and annual percentage yield), transfer limits set by the account terms, and dormancy and fee questions, with a much lower transaction volume.

Other customers hold products Dana does not, and each adds its own operational flavour. A money market deposit account is savings-like with tiered rates by balance and limited transactions, so it brings rate maintenance and change-in-terms notices. A certificate of deposit holds funds for a fixed term at a fixed (or sometimes variable) rate with an early-withdrawal penalty, so it brings maturity notices, rollover processing, a grace period after maturity and interest disbursement options. Retirement and health savings accounts are deposit accounts in a tax-advantaged wrapper with the bank as custodian, which means contribution limits, distribution rules and tax reporting. Custodial, guardianship, estate, trust and escrow accounts are held by one person for another, so the legal owner is not the person who controls the account, and documentation proves authority. Business accounts bring entity documents, authorised signers and resolutions, and public-funds accounts bring collateralisation. Sweep and zero-balance accounts move money automatically every night, so their exceptions are reconciliation problems. The full comparison is in the product catalogue (12.18).

How an account is owned matters as much as what it is. A single account has one owner and passes through the estate or a beneficiary designation on death. A joint account with right of survivorship passes to the survivors, either owner can typically transact, and legal process against one owner may reach the funds depending on state law. Joint without survivorship (tenants in common) gives each owner a share that passes to their estate. In some states, tenancy by the entirety for married couples gives protection from a creditor of only one spouse, which is a legal-process complexity that Legal decides. Payable-on-death, transfer-on-death and in-trust-for designations name beneficiaries who receive funds at death without probate. Accounts held in a representative capacity (trust, estate, custodial, fiduciary) need authority documents and limits, and a business entity owns its account, while individuals are only signers who can change without the entity changing.

The words for the people on an account are a common source of confusion. The owner legally owns the funds and is party to the agreement. An authorised signer may transact but owns nothing. An agent under a power of attorney acts on the owner's behalf within the scope the document and state law allow, and the bank reviews and accepts or declines the document. A beneficiary has no access during the owner's life, and a representative payee manages benefit payments for someone unable to, with funds protected for that person. The role table is in 12.18.

This is the reason the data model carries a many-to-many holder relationship with a role (primary, joint, signer, power of attorney, beneficiary): customer, account and holder are modelled in the canonical data model and read from the core through data pages, and the case stores keys and the delta being requested, not a copy of the account. Titling and role then drive decision tables (who must consent, which documents, which approvals), and the product code drives circumstancing, such as certificate-of-deposit rules against checking rules (Chapter 03 §3.13).

12.4 Four journeys on Riverbend's ledger

With the lifecycle in mind, four events show the rules working on real accounts. Each journey runs trigger, required action, clock, exceptions, evidence, and the later sections give the full rule for each regulation it touches.

Journey 1: a payment arrives

On Wednesday 4 March 2026 a $2,100.00 ACH credit posts to Dana's checking account. Trigger: the bank receives an electronic payment. Required action: make the funds available. Clock: funds must be available no later than the business day after the banking day on which the payment was received, so the deadline is Thursday 5 March 2026. Exceptions: the rule lets a bank place an exception hold in defined circumstances, which is a Compliance-owned decision and a separate case. Evidence: the receipt timestamp, the calendar used, the computed availability date and who or what applied any hold.

Legal requirement. 12 CFR 229.10(b): electronic payments (wire, ACH credit) are available on the business day after the banking day the bank received them, where "received" means the funds were actually and finally collected and the account and amount information was received. Business day here is the Reg CC business day (12 CFR 229.2(g)), a fixed statutory list that excludes Juneteenth and has a one-directional Sunday rule (12.10).
Riverbend policy (assumed, not a legal requirement). Riverbend's design assumption is that electronic credits show as available the same day they post, which is earlier than the legal minimum; the legal date is still computed and stored so that an exception hold or a policy change cannot push availability past it. assumed Riverbend policy

The same payment family has a failure path: if Harlow Freight Co. originates a payment that its receiving bank cannot post, a return comes back, and case PE-30142 handles it (Chapter 07 §7.12). The clock there belongs to Nacha's return time frames, counted in banking days and held as reference data (12.8), and the evidence is the original trace number, the return reason code and the reversal.

Journey 2: a hold

On 9 March 2026 a $200.00 card authorisation sits on Dana's checking account, reducing her available balance from $4,418.55 to $4,218.55 without touching the ledger balance. Trigger: a merchant authorisation. Required action: reserve the funds against the available balance. Clock: the authorisation lapses or settles under the card network's rules, and here it settles at 09:40, when the ledger falls to $4,218.55 and the hold is released. Exceptions: authorisation reversals and partial settlements. Evidence: the hold record with placed and released times. A hold by a legal order is a different animal: it is imposed by a third party, runs on a legal clock and needs dual control to release (12.7). For a federal tax levy, for example, the holding period under IRC §6332(c) is 21 calendar days before funds are surrendered. As an illustration only, a federal levy served on a hypothetical account on Monday 9 March 2026 would be held for 21 calendar days, to Monday 30 March, and remitted on the first business day after the holding period expires, Tuesday 31 March 2026.

Legal requirement. Federal levy: restrain funds on hand at receipt, then hold for 21 calendar days under IRC §6332(c) and Treas. Reg. §301.6332-3 before surrendering, and remit on the first business day after the holding period expires. State levies follow state procedures and periods. A bank hold under the Reg CC schedule is a separate mechanism with its own clocks (12.10).
Riverbend policy (assumed, not a legal requirement). Riverbend's assumption is that a legal-order restraint is placed by a maker and released only with two separate approvals (the privilege split of Chapter 10 §10.3), and that the case SLA runs from the order's response due date. assumed Riverbend policy

Required action: investigate, provisionally credit if the investigation is not finished, and tell Dana the result. Journey 4: a closure, AC-10882

On Monday 9 March 2026 Dana asks to close her checking account. Trigger: a closure request. Required actions, in order: verify the requester; confirm no legal hold or restraint remains (the order of Chapter 05 §5.15 places the hold on the core before disbursement, and a legal order arriving mid-closure raises a ticket on the case, Chapter 02 §2.4); wait for the $200.00 card authorisation to settle at 09:40, since the available balance is $4,218.55 only after it does; disburse the remaining balance by the method the customer chose; cancel services (card, online access, standing instructions); issue the final statement and notice. Clock: the closure SLA is Riverbend's, and the legal clocks that can interrupt it (a legal order, a Reg CC hold on a recent deposit) run on their own calendars. Exceptions: a negative balance, returned items after closure, an active legal hold, a joint holder who has not consented. Evidence: the case history linking the request, the verification, the hold clearance, the maker and checker, the disbursement and the notices.

Legal requirement. Funds under a legal restraint cannot be disbursed on closure until the restraint is cleared; a holding period for a federal levy (21 calendar days, IRC §6332(c)) must run; and a customer's right to close an account is not obstructed or delayed (conduct rules; Chapter 08 §8.6). Record retention for identification records runs for a regulated period after closure and the period is held as reference data.
Riverbend policy (assumed, not a legal requirement). Riverbend's design assumes a closure over a threshold amount needs a second approver, that the final statement is generated at disbursement, and that the case waits for card authorisations to clear before disbursing. The threshold value is held in reference data. assumed Riverbend policy
Check your understanding.

Dana's dispute DS-10915 started on 8 December 2025. A developer computes the 45-day investigation deadline by adding 45 business days. What date do they get, and what is the error?

Answer. Forty-five business days from 8 December 2025 lands well into February 2026 and is later than the legal deadline of Thursday 22 January 2026, which is 45 calendar days from receipt. The regulation says "45 days", not "45 business days", so a bank that adds business days completes late, and the error fails in the direction that harms the consumer. The correct design stores the clock type with each deadline (business or calendar), which depends on reading the clock type from the rule and not assuming it from the duration.

12.5 Account opening, KYC and AML

Before Dana's accounts existed, Riverbend had to know who she was, and the law requires it to keep knowing. Banks are gatekeepers of the financial system: they must identify customers, keep sanctioned and criminal actors out, watch for suspicious activity and report it, and failure carries large fines and consent orders. That is why account opening is controlled, documented and audited, and why even a routine change such as Dana's address change AM-10417 can trigger a targeted review of what the bank knows about her.

The legal frame is the Bank Secrecy Act and its anti-money-laundering requirements. A bank must run a programme with internal controls, independent testing, a designated BSA officer, training and customer due diligence, the commonly cited five pillars. Know-your-customer is the umbrella: identify the customer, verify, assess risk and monitor. The first concrete requirement is the Customer Identification Program from the USA PATRIOT Act. Before opening Dana's account the bank had to collect her name, date of birth, address and an identification number (for a US person, a taxpayer ID), verify her identity within a reasonable time through documents or non-documentary means, keep the records for the regulated retention period, check government lists and give her notice. If the data was incomplete the application waited, with an SLA; if the identity could not be verified in the allowed time it was declined.

Verification is followed by screening. Dana's name was checked against sanctions lists administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control (including the Specially Designated Nationals list), politically exposed persons lists, adverse media and the bank's own watch lists. A possible match goes to a trained analyst who compares identifiers (name, date of birth, address, country) and decides: a false positive, with the reason documented, or a true match, which means blocking the property and reporting to OFAC within the period the sanctions regulations set (Compliance owns that figure) and, above all, never tipping the customer off. Sanctions is a strict-liability regime, so a violation can occur without intent, and some transactions are rejected rather than blocked. A politically exposed person is not automatically prohibited but is higher risk and needs enhanced due diligence and senior approval.

The bank then rates the risk (low, medium or high) from the customer type, geography, products, channels and expected activity, usually in a decision table. The rating drives how deep the due diligence goes and how often the relationship is reviewed. High-risk customers get enhanced due diligence: more documentation, source of funds and wealth, senior approval and closer monitoring. For a legal-entity customer such as Harlow Freight Co. the Customer Due Diligence rule adds beneficial ownership: the bank must identify and verify each individual who owns a significant share (the threshold is defined in the rule and held as reference data) and one individual with significant control, and keep that information current. The outcome is one of four decisions (approve, approve with conditions such as limits and monitoring, decline with the proper notice process, or escalate), after which the account is created in the core, disclosures are delivered, a monitoring profile is set and the next review date is assigned by risk rating. Overrides need dual control, and the audit trail records who cleared which alert and why.

Knowing the customer is not a one-time event. Transaction-monitoring systems flag unusual patterns, and analysts close the alert, escalate it or file a Suspicious Activity Report with FinCEN within a fixed regulatory period after detection (Compliance owns the figure). The existence of a SAR is strictly confidential and must never be disclosed to the customer, which is the offence called tipping off. Cash above a daily regulatory threshold is reported through a Currency Transaction Report within a fixed period, and deliberately splitting cash to avoid the report, called structuring, is itself a crime. Government and inter-bank information-sharing requests must be answered within the set period and kept confidential. Periodic refresh reconfirms information on a schedule set by risk rating, event-driven review is triggered by an address or name change, a new signer, adverse media or unusual activity, and sanctions re-screening runs whenever lists are updated. Most screening and monitoring alerts are false positives, so clearing them efficiently with documented rationale, and tuning the rules under governance, is a large part of the workload. The terms and their shapes are collected in the reference catalogue (12.18).

All of this shapes the design. SAR-related work and sanctions escalations require restricted access: a dedicated case type or stage visible only to the compliance team, no mention in customer correspondence, masking from general search and dashboards, and audit of who viewed it. A Pega engineer designs these as separate, access-restricted case types and not a status on a general case.

12.6 Maintenance transactions

Most of the work in a deposits operation is not opening or closing accounts but changing them, and the largest family by case count is maintenance: Dana's address change is the typical case. Every change carries fraud risk, because account takeover often starts with a change of address, email or phone, so the pattern is the same each time: verify the requester, validate the data, approve by risk, update the core, notify the customer and keep evidence.

Take AM-10417. Dana submitted the change through an authenticated web session, so the requester is already verified; the address is standardised through postal validation; the customer record in the core is updated so that all her accounts follow; and confirmation notices go out. Because a change of address is a common precursor to takeover, the bank applies risk rules (a recent change, the channel, a high-risk customer), notifies the old and the new address and other contact points as a fraud control under its policy, may restrict card replacement or wires for a period afterwards, and flags a bad address if mail comes back. The same request by phone from a recently reset credential would take a different path, with out-of-band verification and manual review.

A beneficiary designation needs the owner authenticated, accurate identification and shares that total 100 percent. A title change often needs a new account with the funds moved. 12.7 Holds, freezes and legal process orders

A hold or restraint restricts funds, and the first question is who decided it. A bank may decide for itself, under its own policy and regulation (a deposit hold on a check under Reg CC, a fraud hold, an administrative hold, a sanctions block, a deceased-owner restriction), with time limits and notice rules set by regulation. Or an external legal authority may decide: a garnishment, a tax levy, a child-support levy, a court freeze, an attachment or a forfeiture seizure. Then the bank has no discretion to ignore it, must follow the order's terms and the applicable law under strict deadlines, and is liable for non-compliance, since it may be made to pay the amount itself. Dana's $200.00 card authorisation is a bank hold; the $1,500.00 hold on Victor Osei's account under order LO-20331 is a legal restraint. Legal process is where state-by-state variation is greatest, so this section explains the shape and the mechanics, and the content of each rule is owned by Legal and held as effective-dated reference data. No exemption amounts or state timelines are given.

An order arrives on a clock the bank did not choose. It may come by mail, courier, fax, email, a legal vendor or the registered agent, and the first duty is to record the exact date and time of receipt, because that can fix legal priority and which funds are attached; the second is to route it at once to the specialist legal queue, since a misrouted legal paper in a branch or contact centre is a classic failure. Intake then asks whether the order is valid (a real issuing court or agency with jurisdiction, the correct bank entity, proper service, signed or sealed, legible, with an amount stated) and whether the bank must honour it (an out-of-state order may need domestication, which Legal decides; a suspicious or fake order is escalated to Fraud and Legal).

Then the bank has to find the right person, and a naive implementation gets this wrong. The debtor is matched by name and identifiers (tax ID, address, date of birth) under strict rules: an exact match proceeds, and no match, multiple matches or near matches go to manual review, because restraining the wrong customer is a serious harm. All the accounts are found: individual, joint, and business accounts where the person is an owner and not merely a signer, with custodial and trust accounts being special cases for Legal. The order is classified (type, jurisdiction, issuing authority, amount, deadlines, and whether customer notice is required, permitted or forbidden), and the restraint is computed: which funds are attached (generally the balance at the time of service, per the order and state law), the amount to hold being the lesser of the balance available and the amount in the order (and any allowed costs), minus protected funds. The restraint is placed in the core, with dual control on placing and on releasing; later deposits are generally not attached for a single bank-account garnishment, though the rules vary, and the restraint is applied to each account until the order amount is covered or the accounts are exhausted.

What the order is worth depends on its type. A garnishment by a judgment creditor directs the bank to hold and later turn over funds belonging to the debtor, and state law governs the response period, which funds are attached, the exempt amounts, notice duties, fees and whether joint accounts are reachable. A tax levy collects an unpaid tax directly: Federal levies: restrain funds on hand at receipt, then hold for 21 calendar days under IRC §6332(c) / Treas. Reg. §301.6332-3 before surrendering, and remit on the first business day after the holding period expires; state levies follow state procedures and periods. A child-support levy is honoured by restraining and remitting per the notice and by responding to data-match requests, with exemption rules that vary. A subpoena compels records and not necessarily a freeze, so the work is validating scope, assessing the customer-notice position, collecting records and producing them within the stated period with a certification. A court-ordered freeze restrains exactly what it says until a release order or end date. Law-enforcement seizure and preservation requests are escalated to Legal and Compliance at once, are typically confidential and may relate to a SAR that must never be disclosed. A bankruptcy filing suspends most collection actions through the automatic stay, so restraints that violate the stay are released or suspended as Legal advises. The comparison of types is in 12.18.

Funds that look attachable may be protected, and getting that wrong is the second naive error. Federal benefit payments such as Social Security, SSI and veterans' benefits are protected from most creditor garnishment: a federal rule requires the bank, on receiving a garnishment order, to review the account over a recent look-back period for benefit deposits and to protect automatically an amount up to the benefit deposits found, without the customer having to act (the look-back period and scope are set by the federal rule and are not stated here). So the bank's systems must identify benefit deposits by the payment codes on ACH credits, compute the protected amount, keep it available to the customer and meet strict internal deadlines after receipt. Many states protect specified categories or fixed amounts of funds, which varies widely, changes by legislation and is implemented as maintained reference data circumstanced by state with effective dates, never hard-coded; some exemptions must be claimed by the customer. Wages once deposited, retirement accounts, and escrow, IOLTA and fiduciary funds (which belong to third parties and are not restrained without Legal review) each need Legal's determination, with the system supplying the data.

Several orders can hit the same account, and the bank must not guess the priority. Legal priority depends on order type and timing: federal tax levies and support orders often have special priority in their own law, ordinary creditors generally attach in the order served, and some states have priority statutes, so receipt time must be recorded precisely and Legal sets the rule. The bank may also hold a right of setoff against an overdrawn account, and its timing relative to legal process is a Legal and Credit matter. If only one joint owner is the debtor, whether the whole account, only the debtor's share, or nothing can be restrained depends on state law and the type of joint tenancy, so operations cannot decide: the case asks the right question and preserves the other owner's rights and notices. If the account has less than the order, the available amount is restrained, and whether later deposits are caught depends on order type and state. Items in the clearing process interact with the amount to restrain, and an account that is closed or does not exist still needs a "no funds" answer by the deadline: the order is never ignored. Amended, release and withdrawal orders arrive later and are tracked by version number (Chapter 07 §7.13). The bank may charge a legal-process fee, in some states within a statutory limit. The tables of competing-order and other rules are in 12.18.

Whether to tell the customer is a decision per order type and jurisdiction. Some laws require notice within a stated number of days with specified content, such as the right to claim exemptions; some orders forbid notice (certain criminal, grand jury and government investigative requests), and notifying would be a violation; many cases have no requirement either way and policy decides. Legal owns the decision, which is implemented as a decision table (NoticeAllowed and NoticeRequired), and the correspondence template varies by state and order type (Chapter 02 §2.9).

After placement the bank waits and monitors. The customer may challenge or claim exemptions; some orders require a wait before remitting, such as the federal levy period; and a release order, an amended order or a bankruptcy notice may interrupt the flow. The bank files the required answer or certification with the court, officer or agency by the response date, stating what was held, or that nothing was found; after the required period it remits funds by check or wire with a transmittal, deducts a permitted fee where allowed and reconciles; and on satisfaction, vacatur, expiry or a release order it releases the restraint, notifies the customer and archives the evidence. 12.8 ACH and the Nacha network

Dana's payroll arrives as ACH credits, such as the $2,500.00 and $2,100.00 payments that posted to her checking account in the first week of March. The Automated Clearing House (ACH) network is the US electronic batch payment system for payroll, bill payments, recurring debits, business payments and many consumer transfers. Payments move in files of entries, not one at a time, and settle through the Federal Reserve (FedACH) or The Clearing House (EPN), the two ACH operators; the organisation Nacha writes the operating rules all participants follow. (The file format and return mechanics are in Chapter 07 §7.12.)

Every entry has parties. The originator initiates the payment with the receiver's authorisation (an employer sending payroll, a utility debiting a customer), its bank is the ODFI (originating depository financial institution), which sends entries into the network and bears warranties, the operator sorts entries and calculates settlement, the receiver's bank is the RDFI, which posts the entry and may return what it cannot or should not post, and the receiver is the account holder. A deposits team is often both: an RDFI for inbound entries to its customers (posting, returns, disputes, stop payments) and an ODFI for entries originated by its business customers such as Harlow Freight Co. (limits, return handling, risk monitoring). An ACH credit pushes funds to the receiver and an ACH debit pulls them under authorisation. The entry has an effective date (when the originator wants it to take effect) and a settlement date (when funds actually move between banks); Same Day ACH settles within defined windows up to a per-payment limit set by Nacha (the figure is not stated here); a prenote is a zero-dollar entry that verifies account details first; and every entry carries a 15-digit trace number, the key that matches returns and corrections to the original (Chapter 07 §7.12). ACH return deadlines count in banking days on the network and operator calendar, a different clock from the Reg E and Reg CC business days (12.10).

The way an entry was authorised is carried in its Standard Entry Class code, and it matters for returns. A PPD entry is a prearranged consumer payment such as payroll or a recurring bill, with the consumer protections and the longer unauthorised-debit window. CCD and CTX are corporate, with a short window for a corporate receiver to claim an entry was unauthorised. WEB and TEL are online and telephone consumer debits with authorisation and validation obligations on the originator. ARC, BOC and POP convert a paper check into an ACH debit, which interacts with stop payments, IAT is international, and RCK re-presents a returned check electronically.

Now follow what happens at the RDFI, because this is where a naive implementation posts something it should have returned. Each entry in the file is validated in order. If the account does not exist the entry is returned R03 (or R04 if the structure is invalid); if it is closed, R02; if it is frozen, under a legal hold or an OFAC block, R16 or handling under the hold rules; if the account type cannot accept the entry, R20; if the customer is flagged deceased, R14 or R15; if a stop payment is on file for the originator and amount, R08; if the authorisation was revoked, R07; and a debit against insufficient available funds is returned R01, or paid into overdraft under the customer's election and policy with a fee. A credit is posted and made available under the funds-availability rules. Returns must leave the RDFI within the deadline, counted in banking days, and what the automatic rules cannot decide becomes work in the exception queue. Later the customer may dispute an already posted debit (R05, R07, R10 or R11), which needs a signed statement and a return within the extended window, while the Reg E error-resolution clock may run in parallel.

The deadline shape depends on the code. R01 insufficient funds, R02 account closed, R03 no account, R04 invalid structure, R08 payment stopped and R09 uncollected funds are short, generally the second banking day after settlement; R29, a corporate customer saying a debit was unauthorised, is short too, because corporate receivers must act quickly. R05, R07, R10 and R11 (unauthorised, revoked, not authorised or not in accordance with the authorisation) carry the extended consumer window set by Nacha (the period is not stated here) and need the consumer's signed statement. R14 and R15 trigger the deceased-account process and federal benefits reclamation, and R16 links to the restraint or sanctions case. The per-code deadlines are held as reference data, not hard-coded; the full code catalogue is in 12.18.

On the other side the ODFI receives the return file (Chapter 07 §7.12), matches each return to the original by trace number, reverses the credit given to the originator's account, notifies the originator and applies the treatment for the reason code, as in case PE-30142. It watches return rates per originator, because large rates trigger reviews and limits, and handles Notifications of Change by updating the originator's stored account data. Return rate monitoring has a precise shape that is often blurred: Originators with high return rates are scrutinised. The unauthorised return rate threshold is 0.5% (reduced from 1.0% in 2015), measured on return reason codes R05, R07, R10, R11, R29 and R51 over the preceding 60 days / two calendar months. Separately, an administrative return rate level of 3.0% (R02, R03, R04) and an overall return rate level of 15.0% trigger Nacha's inquiry process. Note the distinction: 0.5% is a hard threshold with enforcement obligations, while 3% and 15% are levels that open an inquiry rather than an automatic violation. A customer's stop payment on ACH debits gives an R08 return, with Reg E timing for preauthorised transfers (12.10). Late returns need the ODFI's agreement, a dishonoured or contested return is a rare case-based exception, and a written statement of unauthorised debit is retained as evidence; account validation and fraud screening before sending are integration calls with resilience (Chapter 05 §5.9).

12.9 Checks, stop payments, wires, card disputes and instant payments

Not every payment is an ACH entry. Dana's $120.45 check of 3 March, a wire a business customer sends, a debit card purchase and an instant payment each travel on a different network with different timing, different reversibility and different rules for when the bank can stop or recall them. The common thread is that each has an exception path (a stop payment, a recall, a dispute), and that the clock on that path depends on the instrument.

A check involves a drawer who writes it, a payee, a depositary bank where it is deposited and a paying bank where the drawer's account is, and a deposits team deals with both directions. Most checks now clear as images under the Check Clearing for the 21st Century Act, and a substitute check is a legal paper replacement printed from an image, carrying warranties and a consumer expedited-recredit right. The paying bank decides each night whether to pay or return each item, under a midnight deadline in commercial law and an expeditious-return duty in regulation (the exact deadlines are not stated here), and the common return reasons are insufficient funds, account closed, stop payment, signature problems, altered or fictitious items, forged endorsement, duplicate presentment and encoding errors. A stale-dated check need not be paid after a state-law period, a post-dated one may generally be paid on presentment unless the customer gave timely notice, and cashier's, certified and teller's checks are near-cash with next-day availability and a counterfeit-scam dispute risk. Remote deposit capture adds duplicate-presentment risk, and positive pay lets a business send an issued-check file so the bank matches presented items and asks the customer to decide exceptions the same day (Chapter 07 §7.13).

A stop payment is the customer's instruction not to pay a specific check, or for ACH a specific or all debits from an originator. Its central problem is timing: it must be placed before the item is paid, and if the item has already posted it is a dispute, not a stop. Under the Uniform Commercial Code an oral stop is effective for a short period unless confirmed in writing, and a written stop for a longer fixed period, renewable (the periods are state and contract law and are not stated here). The details must be precise (account, check number or range, exact amount, date, payee), because systems match on them, and for an ACH debit the instruction must reach the bank a few business days before the scheduled date to be effective under Reg E (the minimum notice is set in Reg E and not stated here), producing an R08 return. The case is short and mostly synchronous: capture details with strict validation, call the core to check whether the item has posted, place or reject the stop, confirm, charge the fee, schedule a renewal reminder before the six-month expiry and link to any later R08 or paid-item dispute. Its SLA is minutes to hours, the integration is real-time with a priority manual queue if the core is down, and the stop record in the core is the source of truth.

Wires are different because they are final. Fedwire settles each domestic payment individually and immediately within operating hours (customer cut-offs are earlier than the network's), CHIPS nets large-value dollar payments, and SWIFT carries cross-border instructions through correspondent banks, with formats migrating to ISO 20022. Once accepted a wire is generally irrevocable under Article 4A of the Uniform Commercial Code and Federal Reserve rules, so mistakes and fraud are hard to undo and prevention matters: the agreed security procedure (authentication, call-back, dual control) shifts the loss for unauthorised orders to the customer only if the bank followed it, which is why adherence is strict, and business email compromise, in which criminals impersonate an executive or vendor to redirect a wire, is met by call-back verification and by treating beneficiary changes as high risk. Wire exceptions (repair items with missing beneficiary data, OFAC hits, insufficient funds, duplicates, returned wires) have strict same-day deadlines, and a recall asks the beneficiary bank to return the funds, which depends on its cooperation and on the funds still being there, so the sooner the request the better the chance. Consumer international remittance transfers have their own disclosure, cancellation and error-resolution rights under a separate section of Reg E, not stated here. The exception case takes intake from the payment hub, uses a decision table by exception type, has a repair step with maker-checker (dual control on any release of funds), sends OFAC hits to the restricted sanctions queue, and sets an SLA on the same-day wire cut-off as a time-of-day deadline in the bank's time zone (Chapter 02 §2.8); a recall case starts its SLA immediately, sends a template message to the beneficiary bank and follows up, and every money-affecting step uses idempotency keys with a manual fallback (Chapter 05 §5.9).

A debit card purchase is an electronic fund transfer covered by Reg E, so an unauthorised-use or error claim follows the Reg E timelines (12.10). The dispute has two clocks: the regulatory one owed to the customer (investigation, provisional credit) and the card network's own deadlines for chargebacks and representments, which use network reason codes and evidence rules. A fraud claim (the card was compromised) and a merchant dispute (goods not received) need different documentation and reason codes, and an ATM claim (cash not dispensed, a deposit error) needs journal and balancing evidence from the ATM network. Instant payments over the RTP network and FedNow operate around the clock with immediate final settlement, so there is no batch window and exceptions occur at any hour, which makes the 24x7 operating model and monitoring (Chapter 10 §10.13) matter. Credit pushes are irrevocable: a mistake is corrected by a return request or a new payment, not by the originator pulling the money back, so authorised push payment scams are higher stakes and need strong pre-send controls. Requests for payment, return and investigation messages generate their own time-boxed cases, and consumer transfers are generally covered by Reg E error-resolution clocks. The catalogues of check, stop payment, wire, card and instant-payment concepts are in 12.18.

Three concrete traps in those definitions.
  • Reg E's business day is per-institution and includes back-office work. The official interpretation is explicit: if a branch opens on Saturday for teller transactions but does not perform internal functions such as investigating account errors, Saturday is not a Reg E business day for that institution. A bank may also elect whether an abbreviated day counts. So the Reg E calendar is a configuration item per institution, not a holiday feed. A Reg E business day is also the full 24-hour period ending at midnight.
  • Reg CC's statutory list does not include Juneteenth. §229.2(g) enumerates January 1, the third Monday in January, the third Monday in February, the last Monday in May, July 4, the first Monday in September, the second Monday in October, November 11, the fourth Thursday in November, and December 25. Federal Reserve Banks have observed Juneteenth as a holiday since 2021. Drive Reg CC off "the Fed holiday calendar" and your availability schedule is wrong on 19 June.
  • Reg CC's Sunday rule is one-directional. If January 1, July 4, November 11 or December 25 falls on a Sunday, the following Monday is also not a business day. There is no matching rule for a Saturday — so when July 4 falls on a Saturday, the preceding Friday is still a Reg CC business day even though federal offices observe the holiday then.
Design consequence. A single IsBusinessDay helper is a defect waiting to happen. Model the calendar as an input to the deadline, not a global: CalculateDueDate(trigger, ruleId, calendarId, timeZone, cutOff). In Pega terms that means more than one Calendar rule — at minimum a Reg CC statutory calendar, a per-institution Reg E calendar, and separate operational calendars — chosen by the rule being applied rather than by the case type.

12.11 Reconciliation and controls

Every figure in this chapter eventually has to agree with another system's figure, and reconciliation is how a bank proves that it does. The core's end-of-day balances, the payment hubs' settlement totals, the general ledger and the Pega-side records are four views of the same money, and reconciliation is the control of comparing two independent records of the same thing and explaining every difference. An unexplained difference may be an error, a timing delay, a missing item or fraud. The SQL for the core-against-Pega comparison is in Chapter 11 §11.13.

The structure is layered. The general ledger is the bank's official accounting record, and the core system's account balances are the subledger behind each control account, so the sum of all checking balances in the core must equal the GL control account, compared daily, with any out-of-balance condition investigated at once. Items that cannot yet be posted to their proper place wait in a suspense account, which must be cleared quickly because aged suspense is an audit finding. Clearing and settlement accounts (ACH settlement, check clearing, wire settlement with the Federal Reserve) are proved daily so settlement entries equal customer postings, the day's debits must equal its credits in the proof of cash and balancing, and correspondent banking is reconciled through nostro and vostro accounts to statements. A break is any unreconciled difference, such as the core saying 15,230.55 while the ledger says 15,230.00.

Breaks have types, and the type decides the owner and the cure. A timing break resolves itself (one system posted before the other), so it waits and re-matches under aging rules. A missing item exists in one system only and needs the source found, a post or reversal, and an investigation of the interface failure. An amount difference (fees, rounding, a wrong amount) is corrected on the erroneous side with approval. A duplicate is reversed once and investigated as an idempotency failure (Chapter 05 §5.9). An unmatched settlement is traced to specific files or entries, and an out-of-balance condition between the GL and the subledger is the highest priority, since it may be a systemic posting problem. Priya's team sees breaks by age: zero to one business day is auto-match retry, two to five days needs an analyst with an owner assigned and supervisor visibility, six to thirty days is a formal case with root cause and approval to write off or adjust, and beyond thirty days goes to senior review with potential loss recognition and audit visibility (the age bands are illustrative).

Control around the process matters as much as the matching. An adjustment or write-off needs dual control, with authority limits by amount; the person who posts transactions does not reconcile them, and the reconciler cannot post adjustments to the same accounts; reconciliations are completed by a deadline each day and aged items escalated; sign-off, supporting documents and the reviewer are kept as evidence for the retention period; management sees open breaks by age and amount with trends and root causes; internal audit and financial-reporting controls testing review the evidence; and repeat breaks lead to interface or process fixes, not just clearing items. The tables of concepts, types, aging and controls are in 12.18.

As a case, a break is detected by set-based SQL or a staging-and-MERGE pipeline (Chapter 05 §5.8, Chapter 11 §11.13) that loads core balances and ledger balances and computes the differences; each break above a tolerance creates a case with an exposed key (account, business date, amount, age). The stages are Triage (auto-match retry), Investigate (guided by break type, with links to related transactions and files), Resolve (adjustment, reversal or write-off with maker-checker and the authority matrix by amount), Verify (re-reconcile) and Close, with an SLA by age band and escalation, an aged-break dashboard, a root-cause code for trend analysis and evidence attached. The same pattern applies to ACH settlement, wire settlement and suspense account reconciliation.

12.12 Fraud, risk and disputes

Dana's dispute DS-10915 is one of a family: some transactions a customer says they did not make, some were made by a fraudster, some are errors by the merchant or the bank. Distinguishing them matters because each has a different owner, a different clock and, under Reg E, different liability. Fraud is also the reason the controls in the earlier sections exist, from verifying a change of address to placing a hold.

The patterns recur. Check kiting exploits the float between banks by depositing checks drawn on another bank's account and withdrawing before they clear, and shows as repeated large deposits between the same accounts and balances that depend on uncollected items. Mule accounts, often new, receive and quickly move proceeds of crime, with sudden large inbound ACH or wires and rapid outbound transfers. Account takeover begins with phishing, SIM swap or credential theft, usually a change of contact details first, then a password reset, a new payee and large transfers from a new device. Synthetic identities built from real and fabricated data show thin or inconsistent identity data and shared addresses. Check fraud (altered, counterfeit, washed, stolen) shows image anomalies and duplicate presentment; mobile deposit duplicates show duplicate MICR and amount; unauthorised ACH debits come from unknown originators. Business email compromise redirects wires, and authorised push payment scams manipulate the customer into sending money themselves, which Reg E treats differently from unauthorised transfers (a policy and legal question). Elder financial exploitation shows sudden changes in pattern, new signers or a power of attorney, and an accompanying person controlling the transaction; it calls for trained staff, reporting to adult protective services and, where applicable, a SAR, with protective holds only where law and policy permit and no accusation of the customer. First-party and bust-out fraud builds credibility, then exploits it. The responses range from step-up verification and fraud holds to exception holds, EDD, closure and law-enforcement liaison; the pattern table is in 12.18.

An alert is handled in a fixed sequence. Detection (real-time rules, model scores, monitoring or a customer report) creates an alert, triaged by risk score and amount into a queue with same-day clocks; the first action is containment, placing a fraud hold or card block immediately with a reason of FRAUD and notifying; the investigation contacts the customer through a verified channel and reviews activity, device and location and linked accounts; and the decision is either genuine (release the hold and document) or fraud confirmed (reverse where possible, recover, close or restrict, handle the Reg E claim and consider a SAR). The outcome feeds rule tuning and model feedback (Chapter 08). Releasing a fraud hold above limits needs dual control, SAR-related notes are restricted, everything is audited, and customers are treated fairly and promptly because holds harm genuine customers.

Disputes divide by what governs them. An unauthorised or erroneous electronic transfer (debit card, ACH, online) is under Reg E (12.10), with business-day investigation and provisional credit rules, which is Dana's case. A merchant dispute on a debit card purchase runs on card network rules, with chargeback windows, plus Reg E for errors. A check forgery, alteration or duplicate payment is governed by commercial law and check regulations, with notice periods for the customer. A fee dispute, hold dispute or service complaint follows bank policy, consumer-protection regulation and complaint-handling rules, and a wire dispute or recall follows funds-transfer law and the security-procedure agreement with immediate clocks (12.9). The taxonomy table is in 12.18.

As cases this is a family: alert intake from detection systems (Chapter 06, 07), a decision table for priority and routing, a restricted queue, steps that place and release holds and block cards with idempotency and manual fallback, a customer-contact step with a verified-channel rule, a Reg E timer sub-process when a claim applies, links among fraud, ACH, card and Reg E cases on the same account, and dashboards for alert volume, time to contain, loss and false-positive rate. Fraud and SAR-related content is in restricted access (Chapter 10 §10.3).

12.13 Dormancy, unclaimed property and escheatment

If Dana stopped using her savings account and could not be reached, the account would not stay in limbo for ever. When a customer stops using an account and cannot be reached, it becomes dormant (inactive). Under unclaimed property (escheat) laws, after a state-defined period the bank must make reasonable efforts to contact the owner and, failing that, remit the funds to the state, which holds them for the owner to claim; the bank no longer owns or holds them. The rules are state law, so they vary in dormancy period, notice timing, reporting dates and what counts as owner activity, and this section gives the shape and leaves the numbers to reference data owned by Compliance.

The process runs on a timeline. At T0 the owner makes the last owner-initiated activity: deposits and withdrawals the owner initiated, contact with the bank and, in some states, online logins restart the clock, while bank-initiated entries such as interest credits and fees generally do not. After a period set by the product terms the account may become bank-dormant, a status separate from legal escheat that restricts some activity and may carry a dormancy fee where permitted. Before reporting, the bank sends a written due-diligence notice to the last known address, within a window before the state's reporting date, giving the owner the chance to respond; if there is still no owner contact by the report date the property is legally escheatable, an annual report goes to the state (generally the state of the owner's last known address, the holder's state if there is none) and the funds are remitted. Any owner contact before remittance reactivates the account, resets the clock, cancels the escheat item and records why. After escheat the owner can still claim from the state, and the bank supports claim queries with records. Official checks, cashier's checks and certificates of deposit have their own dormancy triggers, such as CD maturity, and shorter or longer periods.

The process interacts with others, and a naive implementation overlooks them. Accounts with active legal process are not escheated, so holds are checked before reporting. Returned mail marks the address as bad and affects the due-diligence evidence, so outreach attempts are documented. A deceased owner changes the process to contact with the estate or beneficiaries. Owner contact reactivates the account, so the case must close and reverse the escheat path, and remittances must tie to the ledger, with unclaimed-property liability accounts reconciled. The shape and interaction tables are in 12.18.

How this becomes a Pega case type: Dormancy and Escheatment. Selection: a Job Scheduler scans accounts by last owner-initiated activity date (exposed, indexed column, Chapter 03.3, 11.13) joined to a state-by-state rules table (dormancy years, notice lead time, report date); it queues chunked items for a dedicated queue processor (Chapter 06.9). Case: one case per account (or per owner and state) with stages: Eligibility check (active holds, legal orders, deceased flag, recent activity, product exceptions); Notice (correspondence by state, with proof of mailing); Wait (to the report date or owner contact); Report and remit (batch file to the state, dual control); Resolved or Reactivated. Rules: state-specific dormancy periods, notice windows and report dates as effective-dated reference data owned by Compliance with circumstancing by state (Chapter 03.10). Controls: reconciliation of selected, noticed, reactivated and remitted counts and amounts; dual control on remittance; audit evidence of outreach attempts. Interrupts: a ticket when the owner makes contact or a legal order arrives (Chapter 02.4).

12.14 Operational metrics: what an operations manager cares about

Priya needs to know whether her queue is healthy without opening every case, and operational metrics are how she knows. Deposit operations are managed by numbers, and they turn the processes above into figures that managers and auditors can read: how many cases arrive, how long they take, how many breach their clock, how many are reworked.

The headline measures are turnaround time (elapsed time from receipt to completion, in business hours or days, reported as average, median and 90th percentile because averages hide long tails), SLA attainment (the share of items completed within the target), and regulatory deadline breaches (a count of items missing a legal clock, with a target of zero and a dedicated control report). Quality shows in first-pass yield (the share completed correctly the first time without rework), the exception rate (the share needing manual handling, a signal of data quality or rule gaps), and the rework and reopen rate. Efficiency shows in the straight-through processing rate (the share fully automated with no human touch), productivity (items per person per day and average handling time), cost per case and queue wait time (how long an item waits unassigned, which exposes capacity or routing problems). Control health shows in backlog and its ageing and in reconciliation ageing and open break amount (12.11), and conduct risk in complaints and escalations. Forecasting needs incoming volume by type and hour and forecast accuracy, for month-end and tax-season peaks. Each is produced from case data: exposed timestamps for turnaround, SLA outcomes captured as exposed fields, an exception-reason code, a rework flag from history and status transitions, and counts of cases resolved with no manual assignment. The metric table is in 12.18.

The arithmetic is simple and worth checking by hand.

Examples: SLA attainment = items completed within SLA / total items completed (for example 9,420 of 9,800 = 96.1 percent) First-pass yield = items with no rework / items completed (for example 8,600 / 9,800 = 87.8 percent) STP rate = items resolved with zero manual assignments / total items (for example 5,200 / 9,800 = 53.1 percent) Little's law check: average work in progress = arrival rate x average cycle time (for example 400 items/day x 1.5 days = 600 open items) Capacity check: required staff = (volume x average handling minutes) / (productive minutes per person per day) (for example 400 x 12 / 360 = 13.3 people)

An operations manager asks three questions every morning: what is breaching or about to breach, where is the backlog ageing, and what is generating rework. The design makes the case data so those answers come from exposed fields (the SLA outcome, the age band, the exception reason and the rework flag) and not from reconstructing them from history later. Regulatory deadline breaches get their own report with a target of zero.

12.15 The case type catalogue: from domain process to Pega design

The lifecycle, the journeys and the regulations come together as a family of case types, and the way to see how is to follow one of Dana's requests through them. Her address change is Account Maintenance. Her closure is Account Closure, which checks for holds, and if a legal order arrives while it is in flight, a ticket interrupts it and the closure case waits behind a Legal Process Order, whose restraint is placed by Hold Placement child cases with idempotency keys and a read-back. Her dispute is a Reg E Dispute with its own business-day and calendar-day clocks, linked to the card or ACH exception that produced it. A payment of Harlow Freight Co.'s that comes back is an ACH or Payment Exception on banking-day deadlines. The reconciliation that proves it all is Reconciliation Break, and the scheduled sweeps produce Dormancy and Escheatment and CD Maturity and Rollover.

Each case type is defined by the same five things: its trigger, the clock that governs it, the data and decisions it needs, the integrations it calls and the controls around it. Maintenance is triggered by a customer or branch request on a business-day SLA with verification-level decision tables and field audit; closure by a request or the bank on an internal turnaround with an eligibility decision and dual control; a legal order by receipt of the order on the order's response date, with strict matching and a restricted queue; a deposit hold review by an exception hold on Reg CC business days with notice timing; a stop payment by a request that must beat posting, with a six-month expiry; a wire exception or recall by a repair item or fraud report on same-day cut-offs; a dispute by a customer claim on the Reg E clocks plus network windows. KYC review, fraud alert review, reconciliation break, dormancy, CD rollover, deceased account and reference data change complete the family. The full grid is in the catalogue in 12.18, and Chapter 13 develops the architecture and one worked blueprint, with the others as variations.

12.17 Interview Q&A

Q1. A garnishment order arrives. Walk me through what happens operationally, and how you would model it.

"Record the exact date and time of receipt, because it can fix priority and which funds are attached, and route it at once to the specialist queue. Validate that it is a real order from a court or agency with jurisdiction and proper service. Identify the debtor with strict matching: an exact key match proceeds, anything fuzzy goes to a person, because restraining the wrong customer is a serious harm. Classify the order and apply the state and type rules: which funds are attached, exempt funds such as protected federal benefits, and whether customer notice is required, allowed or forbidden. Compute and place the restraint, with dual control, then handle notices, any waiting period, the answer to the court by the response date, remittance and release. As a Pega design that is a Legal Process Order case with child hold-placement cases per account, decision tables owned by Legal for routing, exemptions, notice and priority, a dynamic SLA from the response date on the business calendar, and a ticket for amended or release orders. The legal content is Legal's; I build it as maintained, effective-dated reference data."

Q2. What is the difference between a garnishment and a tax levy?

"A garnishment is a judgment creditor's court process: the creditor sued, won a judgment, and the court process directs the bank to hold the debtor's funds. A tax levy is a tax authority collecting directly under its own statutory power, without needing a court judgment first. The shape differs: a federal levy carries a statutory 21 calendar day holding period under IRC §6332(c) before the bank may surrender the funds, which exists so ownership disputes can be settled — the bank remits on the first business day after it expires, the IRS can request an extension, and the depositor can waive it. Federal levies generally have priority over later claims. Garnishments follow state procedure with state-specific response periods and exemptions. In both, the bank restrains the funds on hand at service, answers by the deadline and remits. The state-specific periods and exemption amounts I would always confirm with Legal."

Q4. What happens when several orders hit the same account?

"Priority matters when funds are insufficient. The general principle is that legal priority depends on the order type and timing: certain federal and support orders have special priority in their own law, and among ordinary creditors the order served first generally attaches first, with state-specific rules. So the system must record the receipt time precisely, keep one case per order with relationships between the cases on the same account, and apply a priority decision owned by Legal rather than guessing. There is also the bank's own right of setoff, which is a Legal and Credit decision."

Q6. Explain ACH returns R01, R02, R03 and how deadlines work.

"R01 is insufficient funds, R02 account closed, R03 no account or unable to locate. For these the RDFI must send the return within a short window, generally by the second banking day after settlement, counted in banking days, not calendar days. Unauthorised-debit returns, R05, R07, R10 and R11, have the extended consumer window set by the Nacha rules, and need the customer's signed statement; for a corporate customer, R29 has the short window. The treatment differs by code: R01 may be re-presented under the rules, R02 and R03 must not be; R03 repeated can indicate error or fraud. A return is different from a notification of change, where the RDFI corrects data rather than rejecting the payment. As a design, a decision table by reason code drives the treatment, SLA and notice, with SLAs set at a fraction of the network deadline."

Q8. A customer says an ACH debit was not authorised. What are the timelines and what does the bank do?

"Two clocks may run. The ACH side: for a consumer account the unauthorised-debit return window is extended under the Nacha rules, and requires a signed statement from the customer, giving reason codes like R10; for a business account the window is much shorter, R29. The Reg E side: it is an error claim, so the investigation, provisional credit and reporting clocks run from the customer's notice. So the case captures the notice date, collects the signed statement, returns the entry within the window, and runs the Reg E timers in parallel, linking the ACH exception case and the Reg E dispute case. I would confirm the exact periods with Payments Compliance."

Q9. Describe the Reg E error resolution timeline.

"The shape: the consumer notifies the bank within a set period after the statement showing the error. The bank investigates promptly and determines whether an error occurred within 10 business days. If it needs more time it may extend the investigation to 45 calendar days, but then it must give the consumer provisional credit within the initial 10 business days; longer periods apply for new accounts (20 business days for the first stage) and for transfers not initiated within a state (90 calendar days for the extended stage). It reports results within 3 business days of finishing; if there was an error it corrects it, if not it explains in writing and may reverse the provisional credit with notice. Consumer liability for unauthorised transfers is tiered by how fast they report. In Pega I would drive dynamic SLAs from the notice date on the business-day calendar, with a decision table for provisional credit, and store every date and notice as evidence. Compliance owns the exact figures, and I keep them as reference data."

Q10. How does a Reg CC hold work, and what do you need to build to support it?

"Reg CC limits how long the bank can hold deposited funds. A deposit is treated as made on a business day if it arrives before the bank's cut-off, no earlier than a regulatory minimum. Some deposits, such as cash, electronic payments, bank-issued checks and a minimum amount of other checks, must be available the next business day; other checks have short standard holds. The bank may extend in listed exceptions: new accounts, repeated overdrafts, large deposits above a threshold, redeposited items, reasonable cause to doubt collectibility, emergencies; and when it extends it must send a notice with the account, deposit, amount held, reason and availability date. Most holds are produced automatically in core; Pega handles the manual reasonable-cause holds, challenges and releases. I would model the business-day and cut-off logic with a calendar rule, keep the thresholds as effective-dated reference data, and store the rule version and reason on each hold so a complaint can be answered."

Q13. Explain CIP, CDD, OFAC screening and SAR, and what a case design must respect.

"CIP is the customer identification programme: collect minimum identity data, name, date of birth, address and an ID number, verify within a reasonable time and keep records. CDD adds understanding the customer and, for entities, identifying beneficial owners above the rule's ownership threshold and one control person. OFAC screening checks customers and transactions against sanctions lists; it is strict liability; a true match means blocking the property and reporting within the period the sanctions regulations set. A SAR is the report of suspicious activity to FinCEN within a set period, and its existence is strictly confidential, so it can never be disclosed to the customer. In design: fail closed when screening is unavailable, clear false positives with documented rationale and dual control, and put SAR and sanctions escalations in restricted-access case types with no leakage into customer correspondence, dashboards or general search."

Q16. What is dormancy and escheatment, and why do state differences matter?

"After a state-defined period without owner-initiated activity, an account is dormant, and unclaimed property law requires the bank to send due-diligence notice and, if the owner does not respond, remit the funds to the state, usually the state of the owner's last known address, with annual reports. Periods, notice windows, reporting dates and what counts as activity all vary by state, and automatic bank entries like interest or fees typically do not restart the clock. Accounts with legal holds or deceased owners are handled differently, and owner contact reverses the process. In Pega I would have a Job Scheduler scan by last-activity date joined to a state rules table, create cases with eligibility checks, notice, wait and remittance stages, circumstance by state, keep the periods as Compliance-owned reference data, and reconcile selected, noticed, reactivated and remitted counts."

Q17. A customer asks to close an account that has an active legal hold. What happens?

"The closure cannot complete while the restraint exists. The closure case's eligibility decision checks active holds and legal flags; if a restraint is active, it routes to a hold review stage, tells the customer in compliant, non-disclosing language, because some orders forbid notice, and the closure resumes only after the hold is released through the legal process. If a legal order arrives mid-closure, the order case raises a ticket on the closure to stop it, as in my Legal Process Order and Account Closure designs. I would confirm with Legal what the customer may be told."

A legal order is a ticking clock attached to someone else's money. The domain complexity is in the variation, by order type and by state, which means the architecture must keep every rule as effective-dated reference data owned by Legal. The design makes the case so that the receipt timestamp is captured precisely, the customer match is strict, the restraint is placed by idempotent child cases, the exemptions and notice rules come from maintained decision tables, the deadlines are computed on the business calendar, and release needs two different people. The rules' content is owned by Legal; the application implements and tests them.

12.Z Where we are now

What the application can do

The application can place a payment, a hold, a dispute and a closure on the right clock: a Reg CC availability date on the statutory calendar, a hold released when its authorisation settles, a Reg E dispute with each deadline computed, stored with its inputs and typed as business or calendar days, and a closure that waits for every restraint to clear before it disburses.

The mechanisms behind it

The lifecycle structures the case family; each regulation is a trigger, required action, clock, exceptions and evidence; the calendar is an input to the deadline and not a global; a deadline is a record with its rule version and is never recomputed silently; and legal requirement and Riverbend policy sit in separate blocks so a reader can always tell which is which.

What it still cannot do

The chapters so far have each built one mechanism. The next question is how they combine in one architecture, with the decisions an architect has to defend, the trade-offs between alternatives and what the whole deposits application looks like. That is Chapter 13.
