13.1 What we built, and why every boundary exists

The parts exist. Chapter 01 gave rules that resolve by class, ruleset and circumstance, so Dana's address change AM-10417 could behave correctly for her market. Chapter 02 made it travel through stages, assignments and SLAs. Chapter 03 let the case read account data it does not own. Chapter 04 gave Marcus and Priya two UI paths and a DX API. Chapter 05 connected the case to the core and broke the connection three ways. Chapter 06 followed an event from a topic to a persisted snapshot. Chapter 07 took in files and email, Chapters 08 and 09 added decisioning and a robot for a screen with no API, Chapter 10 secured, shipped and diagnosed it, Chapter 11 looked at the database, and Chapter 12 explained the money and the law. The question this chapter answers is how they fit together and why they are separated where they are, because a boundary that is not justified by a failure is just ceremony.

Each boundary below is stated with the failure that appears when it is removed.

The integration boundary. Pega never calls the core directly and never binds to its payloads, and it talks to everything through the ESB or managed file transfer. Without that boundary every case type contains core-specific calls, so a change to the core's contract or a slow core call is a change to, and an outage of, every flow that touches it. With it, the timeouts, the idempotency key, the one retry owner and the error classification live in one integration framework, and the core's failure is contained at one place (Chapter 05). The ESB also gives the one place to throttle, authenticate and trace by correlation ID, which is what let Chapter 10 reconstruct AC-10882-HOLD-1 across three systems.

The canonical model and its translation point. Between the vendor payload and the case sits a canonical model, and a mapping at the integration boundary translates one to the other. Without it, a renamed field in the core's response breaks forty rules that happened to read it, and a second core version means a second copy of every flow. With it, a vendor change touches one transform and its unit test, unknown codes become an explicit Unknown with an alert instead of a silent default, and the case stores keys, deltas and decisions, never a copy of the account. The translation must sit at the integration boundary, in the integration rules, not in the case flow: if the mapping is in the flow, the flow is again coupled to the vendor.

The layered ruleset stack. Rules are organised in enterprise, framework, implementation and application layers with dependencies that run one way. Without it, the first case type is built in one ruleset and by the third, rules are copied because nothing is shared; by the sixth, a change to a correspondence header edits forty rules; a state's variation needs another copy; and nobody can say which rules are safe to change. With it, shared frameworks sit low, true differences sit high, decision data changes without a code release, and a hotfix is not a full release (Chapter 01).

The case-type split. Maintenance, closure, legal order, payment exception, dispute and the rest are separate case types sharing frameworks, linked by keys, and not one giant case. Without the split, one case carries every process's stages and every clock, a legal order cannot interrupt a closure cleanly, and access cannot be restricted for sanctions and legal work. With it, each has its own clock, authority and access, and interacts with the others through tickets and child cases (Chapter 02, Chapter 12).

The asynchronous boundaries. Writes that matter go through an outbox and a queue processor, long operations through a callback and a sweeper, and notices through a queue. Without them, a connector call sits inside a transaction holding locks for the length of a network wait, so a slow partner becomes a platform stall (Chapter 11 §11.9), and a lost response leaves the case unable to know whether to act again. With them, the decision and its intent commit locally in milliseconds and the effect completes on someone else's clock with an honest status in between.

The reference-data boundary. Rules own policy and data owns numbers. Without it a threshold or a calendar needs a developer release and cannot be effective-dated, so reprocessing an old order uses today's rule. With it, Legal and Compliance change values through a maker-checker case with effective dates (Chapter 03, Chapter 12). And the audit boundary: every decision records the rule version, its inputs and the approver, because evidence reconstructed afterwards is evidence that can be challenged.

With the boundaries stated, the remaining sections set the scene, justify each boundary in detail, and then run a single request through all of them.

13.4 The integration boundary: a pattern per interface, and why

The integration boundary is where most of a deposits platform's incidents arise, so it is where the design effort goes first. There are twenty-eight interfaces in the inventory (13.R1), and they do not each get a bespoke design: each gets one of five patterns, chosen by one piece of logic. SYNC is a request-reply REST call through the ESB. SYNC+Q is a synchronous attempt with queued retry. ASYNC-CB is an asynchronous request with a callback and a status check. EVENT is event-driven intake. BATCH is a file exchange with a trigger file and reconciliation. The reason for having few patterns is operational: a small support team can learn how five patterns fail and recover, and cannot learn twenty-eight.

The logic starts with who is waiting and what a repeat would do. A person looking at a screen needs a safe read now, so reads are synchronous and cached in a data page with an as-of stamp, and a failed read degrades to the cached value, because repeating a read costs nothing. A write that must not be lost or duplicated cannot be synchronous alone: if the call times out, the caller does not know whether the effect happened, so the write is an attempt with an idempotency key, queued for retry through an outbox, with the unknown outcome as its own status (Chapter 05 §5.9). An operation that takes long or spans several systems cannot hold a user thread, so it is asynchronous: the request returns a tracking ID, the answer arrives by callback, and a sweeper covers the callback that never comes. Continuous change that others need quickly is an event, if the bank has an event backbone, because polling either wastes calls or arrives late (Chapter 06). Volume and vendor standards, such as ACH return files and core extracts, are batch with a trigger file, because a file is a durable, hashable artefact that can be registered, staged and reconciled by counts (Chapter 07). Compliance controls such as sanctions screening fail closed, so the case waits and never approves by default. And where there is no interface at all the answer is to ask for one, using a robot only as a time-boxed bridge (Chapter 09).

Two details of the pattern make it hold. Timeouts are agreed at each hop and the ESB's is shorter than Pega's, so the layer closest to the core fails first and reports. And exactly one layer owns retries: if Pega retries and the ESB also retries, one failure becomes a multiplied load on a core that is already struggling. These are written into a one-page contract per operation, agreed with the ESB team before anyone builds, because most integration incidents are boundary disagreements and not code defects. The twenty-eight interfaces and the pattern, reason and resilience note for each are in the reference inventory (13.R1); in the walkthrough of 13.7, the balance read is SYNC, the settlement is EVENT, the hold is SYNC+Q with an outbox, the disbursement is ASYNC-CB and the nightly comparison against the core is BATCH.

The decision logic behind the choices

PATTERN SELECTION (apply per interface) Is a human waiting on screen and is the operation a safe READ? -> SYNC (cached via data page; degraded mode defined) Is it a WRITE that must not be lost or duplicated, with business tolerant of seconds-to-minutes? -> SYNC+Q (outbox, idempotency key, queued retry) Does the backend take long, or involve several systems, or finish on its own clock? -> ASYNC-CB (202 + callback + status sweeper) Does the source produce continuous change that others need quickly and an event backbone exists? -> EVENT (keyed, idempotent, replayable) Is it high volume, once-a-day, or an industry/vendor file standard? -> BATCH (trigger file, registry, staging, reconcile) Is it a compliance control (sanctions, dual control)? -> fail CLOSED; design the manual fallback first Is there no interface at all? -> ask for one; RPA as a time-boxed bridge only (Chapter 09) ALWAYS: correlation ID; idempotency key on writes; timeouts agreed with the ESB (ESB shorter than Pega); one layer owns retries; errors classified at the mapping boundary

13.5 The layered application, and the "everything in one layer" trap

The closure of 13.7 will use rules from five layers without naming them: its flow and eligibility table from the deposits framework, the correlation ID and idempotency handling from the enterprise integration framework, the notice template from the correspondence framework, the product-specific differences from a thin implementation layer, and the portal Marcus sees from the application layer. The structure chosen in the first sprints is the structure the team lives with for years. Chapter 01 gives the mechanics (classes, rulesets, built-on stacks, rule resolution); this section applies them to a deposits programme.

The trap

A new team builds quickly in one application and one ruleset, with every class under one implementation node. It works for the first case type. By the third, rules are copied between case types because nothing is shared; by the sixth, a change to the correspondence header means editing forty rules; a second business unit or state needs different behaviour and the only option is another copy; the authority matrix is embedded in three flows; an integration mapping change forces a full release of the case flows; and nobody can say which rules are safe to change. The cost is not visible until the second year.

13.7 One request end to end: Dana's closure, AC-10882

The boundaries are now justified one at a time, and the test of an architecture is whether they hold together under one real request. Dana Whitfield (CUS-4417) has decided to close her checking account CHK-8842-0117 and move the money to her savings account SAV-8842-0233. She phones Riverbend on Monday 9 March 2026, and Marcus Reed opens the closure in the agent desktop. The case is AC-10882, its class is FCB-Dep-Work-AccountClosure, and Priya Raman is the checker. This is the only journey in the pack that crosses every boundary: the screen, the case engine, data pages, the integration to the core with an unknown outcome, an asynchronous confirmation by event, a disbursement completed on the core's clock, and an audit trail. For each hop the walkthrough states what triggered it, which rule runs and on what page or class, what data enters, what changes, what lives only on the clipboard and what is committed, whether it is synchronous or asynchronous, what Marcus sees, and what happens when that hop fails. The mechanics of the connector, the event path and the authorisation surface are in Chapters 05, 06 and 10; this section only shows how they connect. The times and thresholds are illustrative and carry assumed Riverbend policy where they are Riverbend's own targets.

Hop 1: Marcus submits the closure from the screen

Trigger. At 10:05 Marcus completes the closure form in Constellation (Chapter 04) and clicks Submit. What runs. The browser calls the case engine's DX API, which validates the request against the view and the data model, and creates the case. The case class inherits from the deposits framework; the closure case type's stages (Intake, Pre-closure checks, Approve, Fulfil, Resolve) come from the layer that owns closure, and the authority table and the closure-eligibility table come from the reference-data ruleset, not from the case. What data enters. From the form: the closure reason, the delivery method (internal transfer to SAV-8842-0233), and the requester's verification result (Dana was verified on the call by Marcus against the customer record). From the call context: AccountNumber and PartyID. What is committed. In one database transaction: the case row (with the exposed columns for status, stage, account number and due dates), the first history entry, the correlation ID AC-10882 and the first assignment. Sync or async. Synchronous, because Marcus is waiting. What Marcus sees. The case ID and the Intake stage. If it fails. If the request fails validation, the field-level message appears on the form and nothing is committed. If the response is lost in the network and Marcus clicks Submit again, the DX call's idempotency key returns the same case instead of creating AC-10883 (Chapter 04 §4.6). A double submit is a hop-1 problem, solved at hop 1.

Hop 2: the case reads what it does not own

Trigger. The Pre-closure checks stage needs the account's balance, holds, pending items, linked products and accrued interest. What runs. Data pages, not copies: D_Account[AccountNumber] returns the ledger and available balance, D_Holds returns active holds, and an accrued-interest data page returns interest accrued but not yet paid to closure date. Each is a thread-scope page whose source is a connector to the ESB, mapped to the canonical account class so that no vendor field name appears in the case (13.6). What data enters. At 10:06 the ledger balance is $4,218.55 and the available balance is $4,218.55, because the $200.00 card authorisation settled at 09:40 and the hold that carried it was released. Accrued interest is $0.00, since a CHK01 product pays none; for a savings product it would be a non-zero amount added to the disbursement. What changes. Nothing in the case yet beyond the decision outcomes: the eligibility decision (holds, legal flags, pending items, negative balance, linked products) returns Proceed. The balance used for the approval is copied onto the case as a snapshot, because Priya's approval must record what she saw; the account itself is never copied. Clipboard or committed. The data pages are clipboard-only and are re-read on a defined refresh; the snapshot and the decision log entry are committed. Sync or async. Synchronous, cached. What Marcus sees. The balance with its as-of time. If it fails. If the core read times out, the interactive path does not retry; a read is safe to repeat, so the failure is a read timeout and not an unknown outcome, and the case shows the cached value with its as-of stamp and a banner, or waits (Chapter 05 §5.9, Chapter 03). The case never proceeds on a value it cannot date.

How the balance got to $4,218.55 is itself a hop in an earlier request. The settlement of the $200.00 authorisation reached Pega at 09:40 as event EVT-55121 (sequence 882, partition 3, topic rb.deposits.account.balance.v1, key CHK-8842-0117), and the consumer persisted it behind a guard so that a late duplicate of EVT-55120 could not overwrite it (Chapter 06). The data page re-reads from the persisted snapshot or from the core on its refresh strategy. The point for the architecture is that the closure case does not know or care how the balance arrives; the boundary hides it.

Hop 3: maker, checker, and the authorisation surface

Trigger. At 10:10 Marcus submits the closure for approval (stage Approve). What runs. The approval subprocess, with the authority decision keyed by balance band, routes the assignment to the checkers' queue. Four independent layers keep Marcus from approving his own work (Chapter 10 §10.4): routing excludes him from the queue, an Access Deny on the ApproveClosure privilege applies when the maker equals the current operator, a server-side validation on the Approve flow action rejects maker equals approver even on an API call, and a daily report of maker equals checker should return zero. What changes. At 10:20 Priya opens the assignment, sees the balance snapshot and approves. The approval records her operator ID, the balance she approved against, and the rule version of the authority table. Committed. The assignment completion, the history entry and the decision log are committed in one short transaction. Sync or async. Synchronous. What Marcus sees. Status Pending-Approval, then Approved. If it fails. If Marcus tries to approve his own submission, the refusal is the rule's and not the screen's, and the denial is written to the security log. If no eligible approver exists the supervisor's queue is alerted. If the balance has moved beyond a threshold in reference data between approval and fulfilment, a re-approval rule applies before any money-affecting call is made.

Hop 4: the hold call, a timeout, and an unknown outcome

Trigger. At 10:21 the case enters Fulfil. The first money-affecting step places a hold on the funds to be disbursed so that nothing can leave the account between the decision and the posting: AC-10882-HOLD-1, for $4,218.55. What runs. The integration framework, in the pattern SYNC+Q with an outbox (Chapter 05 §5.15). In one local transaction Pega sets the case status to Pending-Core, writes the history entry, and writes an outbox row carrying the idempotency key AC-10882-HOLD-1 and the hold request, and commits. A queue processor then claims the outbox row and calls the ESB with the key in the header and the correlation ID; the ESB calls the core. Why it is built this way. The decision and its intent commit together, so there is no state in which the case believes it asked and nothing will ever send the request. And the connector call is made outside any transaction that holds locks, so a slow core does not hold a database row (Chapter 11 §11.9). What Marcus sees. Pending-Core, which is an honest statement: the decision is taken and the effect is not yet confirmed.

The failure. At 10:21:08 the call times out: the connector's read timeout elapsed with no response. This is the case the whole of Chapter 05 prepared for. A timeout on a write that may have succeeded is not a transient error; it is an unknown outcome. The core may have created the hold and the response may have been lost, or the request may never have arrived. The queue processor therefore does not re-post. It moves the outbox row to Unknown, leaves the case in Pending-Core, and asks for the status of the key with a lookup call, GET by AC-10882-HOLD-1. At 10:21:15 the ESB answers that the request is in flight at the core. The case stays in Pending-Core and the processor schedules another lookup with backoff. What the processor must not do is send a new request with a new key, because the key identifies the intent and not the attempt; a new key would be a second hold request, and the account could end up with two restraints for one closure. Marcus sees Pending-Core with an attempt count and, on the case, the note that the outcome is being confirmed.

Other failures at this hop have their own handling. If the core rejects the request, the rejection is classified at the mapping boundary: a business rejection (a restraint already present, for instance) becomes a fix assignment carrying the reason; a duplicate key returns the stored response with the same hold reference (the 409 pattern of Chapter 05); a transient server error retries with jittered backoff by the one layer that owns retries. If the ESB or core is down for a sustained period, a breaker opens, the case stays in Pending-Core with a banner and an alert, and a manual fallback applies: a priority queue where an authorised person places the hold directly in the core and records the reference, with dual control, so a closure is not blocked by an outage while the restraint is never skipped.

Hop 5: the confirmation arrives as an event, on partition 3

Trigger. At 10:21:41 the core finishes creating the hold, and the account's change is published as event EVT-55122: sequence 883, partition 3, topic rb.deposits.account.balance.v1, key CHK-8842-0117. The key is the account number, so it lands on the same partition as EVT-55121 and arrives after it, which preserves the order the guard relies on (Chapter 06 §6.2). It carries the full state of the account: ledger balance $4,218.55 and available balance $0.00, with the hold reference. What runs. A data flow consumes the topic, a strategy keeps the event if its as-of is newer than the stored snapshot, and the consumer persists it atomically with its dedupe record. A listener on the persisted change raises the confirmation for any case with an outbox row in Pending-Core or Unknown for that account. What changes. The queue processor's next status lookup also returns the hold reference, and the two sources agree: the outbox row for AC-10882-HOLD-1 moves to Confirmed, the case moves from Pending-Core to the next step, and the hold reference is stored on the case. Committed. The snapshot and the dedupe record in one transaction, then the outbox row and the case status in another. Sync or async. Asynchronous; neither Marcus nor the queue processor waits for it. What Marcus sees. The case leaves Pending-Core, with the hold reference shown.

This is the hop that shows why the unknown outcome had an exit. The timeout at hop 4 did not leave the case guessing: two independent signals, the status lookup by key and the event for the same account, can both resolve it, and either alone is enough. If the event is duplicated, the dedupe boundary ignores it; if it arrives out of order, the snapshot guard refuses it; if the consumer crashes before acknowledging, the offset has not advanced and the event is redelivered; and if the topic backs up, the status lookup answers first. If neither signal arrives within the threshold, the sweeper (hop 6) raises it. The replay tooling is not an option here: Pega cannot reprocess a Kafka dataset by resetting offsets on the consumer group, so replay is something that is built (Chapter 06 §6.3).

Hop 6: disbursement, completed on the core's clock

Trigger. With the hold confirmed, the Fulfil stage re-fetches the balance (a read that is deliberately not cached for this step) and posts the transfer of $4,218.55 from CHK-8842-0117 to SAV-8842-0233, closes the account in the core and cancels linked services. What runs. The pattern is ASYNC-CB: the processor posts the request with the idempotency key AC-10882-DISB-1; the ESB returns 202 with a tracking ID; the case moves to Awaiting-Core-Confirmation with a deadline; and the core completes the posting in its end-of-day cycle. At 02:14 on Tuesday 10 March, the core calls a Service-REST endpoint on Pega with the tracking ID and the result. The endpoint authenticates the caller with a certificate, runs under a narrow service access group that can complete only this kind of assignment, is idempotent on the tracking ID, and commits the case update in a short transaction. What changes. The checking account is closed in the core, the savings balance becomes $12,040.00 + $4,218.55 = $16,258.55, the hold is released with the closure, and the notices are queued. What Marcus sees. Awaiting-Core-Confirmation through the evening, then Disbursed on Tuesday morning.

If it fails. If the callback never arrives, a sweeper scheduler finds cases in Awaiting-Core-Confirmation past their threshold and asks the ESB for the status by tracking ID; the case does not wait for ever. If the callback arrives twice, the second changes nothing. If the core rejects the disbursement (an item arrived in the interval, or a restraint was placed), the case returns to the fix queue with the reason. And if a legal order for the account arrives while the case is in flight, a ticket interrupts it, posting stops, and the closure resumes only after Legal clears it (Chapter 02 §2.4, Chapter 12 §12.7); that is why the hold of hop 4 is placed before money moves and not after.

Hop 7: notices, resolution, and the audit trail

Trigger. The confirmation moves the case to Resolve. What runs. The correspondence framework queues the closure confirmation and the final statement through a queue processor, attaches a copy to the case, handles bounces and prevents mail loops (Chapter 07 §7.16). The notice is queued and not sent inline, so a mail gateway failure cannot undo a closed account. At 07:40 on Tuesday 10 March, the morning reconciliation compares the core's end-of-day balance for CHK-8842-0117 (closed, zero) and SAV-8842-0233 ($16,258.55) with the Pega-side summary using the full outer join of Chapter 11 §11.13, finds no break, and the case is marked Closed. What is now in the audit trail. The history of every status change; the decision log for each rule (rule name, version, inputs, outcome); the maker and the checker with the balance at approval; the idempotency keys AC-10882-HOLD-1 and AC-10882-DISB-1 with the core references and attempt counts; the correlation ID that finds each call in the ESB and core logs; the notice copy and its proof of dispatch; and the reconciliation result. None of it was assembled afterwards: it is the by-product of the design, which is the meaning of "evidence by default". What Marcus sees. Status Closed on Tuesday 10 March 2026 and the case history.

The correlation ID makes the middle of this journey inspectable. A single search across the Pega, ESB and core logs for AC-10882-HOLD-1 reconstructs the hold's timeline: queue item picked, request sent, the ESB's receipt and key, the core's lock wait, the 504 at the ESB's backend timeout, the Unknown status in Pega, the lookup, the late creation of the hold, and the stored response returned for the key (Chapter 10 §10.10).

13.8 The other case types, as variations on the closure

The closure journey is the worked case, and the other case types reuse its machinery: the layers, the shared frameworks, the integration patterns, the audit approach. Each is therefore described only by what differs, with the reason, and the full stage tables are in 13.R2. The question for each is the same four: what is its clock, who has the authority, which integration is different, and what is the evidence.

Legal process order, LO-20331 (Victor Osei, CHK-8842-0719). It differs from the closure because a statutory hold cannot be reversed by an operator and the clock is externally imposed. The clock is the order's own: a response due date, a waiting period before remitting (for a federal levy, 21 calendar days under IRC §6332(c), Chapter 12 §12.7), and a same-day restraint, all computed and stored on the case with the exact receipt time, which can fix legal priority. The authority is Legal's and not Operations': a restricted queue, strict customer matching in which only an exact key match proceeds, and two distinct privileges so that placing and releasing the restraint need different people. The integration differs in that one order creates one Hold Placement child case per account, each with its own idempotency key and the hop-4 pattern, and intake is a mix of a vendor file, scanned mail and email (Chapter 07), none of which a closure has. The evidence adds the receipt time, the match confidence, the exemption outcome, the notice decision and the response, and it is why this case type is built last, behind controls already proved, and run in parallel before cutover (13.12).

Payment exception, PE-30142 (Harlow Freight Co., CHK-8842-1055). It differs because the inbound trigger is a file and the deadline is a network window. The ACH return arrives in a file through the trigger-file handshake of Chapter 07 §7.12, is registered, staged and applied in chunks, and a case is created only where a person must act. The clock is the ACH network's banking-day deadline for the return reason, counted on the network calendar, which is not the Reg E or Reg CC calendar (Chapter 12 §12.8). The authority is a reason-code treatment table, with approval only for large amounts and fee overrides. Duplicates are prevented by the registry on file name and hash, and by an idempotent apply keyed on the original and return trace numbers; the evidence is the trace pair, the reason code and the control totals that prove declared equals staged equals applied plus exceptions plus rejects.

Dispute, DS-10915 (Dana, oral notice Monday 8 December 2025). It differs because provisional credit is a regulatory obligation with its own clock. The clock is Reg E: 10, 3, 2 and 1 business days, and 45 calendar days, computed on the institution's own calendar and stored as a record with its inputs, so that Dana's determination or provisional credit is due Monday 22 December 2025 and the investigation by Thursday 22 January 2026 (Chapter 12 §12.4). The authority is a provisional-credit decision with approval for denial. The integration is a credit posting through the same outbox as hop 4 and a link back to the ACH or card exception that produced the claim, and the evidence is the timer record for each clock, the notices and the investigation file. The difference from the closure is that it has two clocks running to the customer and, for a card, a third to the network.

Account maintenance, AM-10417. It differs by being the light case: a business-day SLA, no regulatory clock beyond notice rules, risk-tiered verification from a decision table on channel and recent change, and a core update that uses the hop-4 outbox with a lighter consequence. It is the pilot case type for that reason (13.12). KYC and AML review adds restricted access, fail-closed screening integrations and a risk rating decision, and the case types visible only to Compliance. Reconciliation break is detected by the set-based comparison of Chapter 11 §11.13, and resolved with maker-checker adjustments and an age-banded SLA. Dormancy and CD maturity are scheduler-driven, selecting keys and queueing chunks, with state-specific timers held as reference data. None of these needs its own architecture: each is a different clock, authority, integration and evidence on the same frame.

The dependencies among them determine the build order, and the order is itself an architectural decision, because the later case types lean on controls the earlier ones prove.

13.10 Scale, failure, deployment and recovery

The walkthrough was one request on a Monday morning. The design's real test is what the same path does at ten times the volume, when a hop fails, when the code behind it changes, and when the platform itself is lost. Scale: what happens at ten times the volume

Dana's closure is one of roughly two hundred a day in the worksheet below. At ten times that, the first thing that does not scale is not the case engine, whose creation rate is under one case per second, but the things the closure leans on. The core's hold and disbursement calls are the first: a queue processor sending two thousand holds with a bounded thread count will queue behind the core's own capacity, which is the reason hop 4 is queued with throttling and not synchronous, and the reason a separate queue processor serves each downstream dependency, so a slow core cannot starve the notices. The second is the database: the closure's exposed, indexed columns keep the supervisor dashboard cheap, but a Report Definition that filtered on an unexposed column would scan the case table, as in Chapter 10 §10.13. The third is the event path: the consumer's partition parallelism and the dedupe table's growth set how fast balances can be absorbed (Chapter 06 §6.8). What scales cleanly is the stateless web tier, by adding nodes. The worksheet shows the arithmetic.

Recovery: when the core's confirmation never arrives, and when the platform is lost

Two different losses need two different answers. The first is the one the walkthrough already contains: AC-10882 is in Awaiting-Core-Confirmation and the callback never comes. Nothing is lost, because the intent is in the outbox with its key and the case is waiting with a deadline; the sweeper asks the ESB for the status by tracking ID, and if the core answers that the posting completed, the case moves on, while if the core has no record of the request, the same key is re-posted. If the status is still unknown at the second threshold the case goes to a human queue with the correlation ID, and the nightly reconciliation of Chapter 11 §11.13 would in any case surface a closed account in the core that Pega still shows open. The second loss is the platform. If the site hosting Pega fails at hop 6, the case row and the outbox are in the database and recover to the replica; the hold and the transfer are in the core, whose own recovery is coordinated; the callback and the sweeper then reconcile what happened in between. Suppose the new primary is ten minutes behind and shows AC-10882-HOLD-1 pending although the core holds it: the processor re-sends with the same key, and the core or ESB returns the stored response with the existing hold reference, so there is one hold. That works only if the key's retention at the ESB outlasts the recovery time, which is why key retention is a stated line in the integration contract. Failure: which part of the closure fails first, and what contains it

Failure was examined hop by hop in 13.7, and the topology is where the containment is physical. If the core is slow, only the queue processor that serves the core is affected, not the web nodes, because heavy and dependency-bound work never runs on interactive nodes. If the ESB is down for four hours, reads degrade to cached values with an as-of stamp, writes sit in outboxes with idempotency keys behind a breaker, sanctions screening fails closed so onboarding waits, legal restraints use the manual fallback with dual control if a deadline is at risk, and on recovery the queues drain at a throttled rate, exhausted items go to a human queue, and counts are reconciled between Pega and the core. If a node is lost, stateless web and background tiers are replaced and the single active listener or queue processor moves. If the database is slow, the symptom is pool exhaustion, which Chapter 10 §10.13 diagnosed from the widest evidence to the narrowest. In each case a stated degraded mode and a manual fallback were agreed in advance with Compliance, because the SLA treatment during an outage is a business decision. Deployment: how a change reaches the closure path without downtime

A change to the closure flow reaches production through the pipeline of Chapter 10 §10.11: one artefact built from the product rule and promoted through QA, UAT, staging and production, with guardrail, PegaUnit, role-access and performance gates, dual control on the production approval, and the release evidence kept. Web and background nodes are replaced by rolling restart, with a cache refresh step, so there is no downtime window; queue processors drain or tolerate the new payload because queue item payloads are versioned. A change to a decision table, such as the closure authority bands, travels the separate faster reference-data path through a maintenance case with maker-checker and an effective date. A schema change is released by expand and contract, so that the previous release still works if the new one is rolled back, and a risky behaviour change is guarded by a feature toggle that can be turned off without a deployment. In-flight cases matter: an AC-10882 created under the old flow must complete under it, so stages and flow shapes in active use are deprecated before they are removed. 13.12 Migration and rollout

Replacing a bank's manual and fragmented processes is a migration as much as a build, and the closure shows why: a case in flight at cutover has a hold in the core and a clock on the case, and neither can be lost. The approach is to prove the platform on a low-risk slice, run old and new side by side with an exit rule, and never leave an in-flight regulatory item without an owner.

Principles

  • Prove the platform and the controls on a low-risk slice before the high-risk processes.
  • Run the new and old side by side long enough to compare results, with a clear exit rule.
  • Never migrate what you can finish in the old system; never leave in-flight regulatory items without an owner.
  • Every cutover has a rehearsed rollback and a named decision owner.
  • Legacy decommission is a planned step, not an assumption.

13.Z Where we are now

What the application can do

Dana's closure AC-10882 runs from Marcus's screen to a confirmed disbursement on Tuesday 10 March 2026, crossing every boundary the earlier chapters built, with each hop's mode, commit point, visible state and failure behaviour known. The hold call timed out, the outcome was unknown, and two independent signals, the status lookup by key and the event EVT-55122, resolved it without a second hold. The other case types reuse the same machinery and differ only in clock, authority, integration and evidence.

The mechanisms behind it

Boundaries justified by the failures they prevent: the integration layer, the canonical model with its translation at the boundary, the layered rulesets, the case-type split, the asynchronous hops and the reference-data and audit boundaries. A pattern per interface from one piece of logic and a contract per operation; shared frameworks built once; sizing, topology and recovery derived from stated assumptions; governance that enforces standards mechanically; and a phased rollout with parallel runs.

What it still cannot do

An architecture does not decide whether someone can explain it. Knowing why the hold uses the same key on the retry, why the translation sits at the boundary, or why Reg E counts business days for ten and calendar days for forty-five is understanding; being able to say it in a few sentences, with one example and the honest limit of what was done, is a different skill. 13.R2 Reference: the full case blueprints

These are the stage-by-stage blueprints behind 13.7 and 13.8, kept together for lookup. Each blueprint gives: purpose and triggers, data and decisions, a stage table with steps, routing, SLA, integrations, correspondence, audit points and failure handling, then the reporting and open questions. Common conventions for all six. Case ID with class-group prefix; CorrelationID created at intake and sent on every call; idempotency key = case ID + step + sequence on every write; every status change writes history; decisions write a decision-log entry (rule version, inputs, outcome); maker-checker enforced in four layers (Chapter 10.4); notices through the correspondence framework with copy attached; SLAs on the correct calendar (Chapter 02.8); dashboards from exposed columns (Chapter 03.3).
